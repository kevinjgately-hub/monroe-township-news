Planning Board · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's October 22 Planning Board meeting

A flag lot on Old Texas Road is split for two homes after neighbors win a list of conditions, a warehouse on South Middlesex Avenue gets 3,654 rooftop community-solar panels, and a drug maker's underground solvent tanks are carried to December.

Oct 22, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel · 7 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Planning Board, regular monthly meeting
Where
Monroe Township Municipal Building, Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Thursday, October 22, 2026, 6:30 p.m.; the livestream runs about 2 hours 16 minutes, with the meeting itself starting about 10 minutes in
Who was there
Chairman Marc Gaffrey, Vice Chairman David Rothman, Councilman Michael Markel, the Mayor's representative (Hemant Patel) and other members, plus the board's attorney, engineer and planner. The captions don't capture the roll call; names are as listed on the township's agenda.

The Planning Board reviews larger development proposals and checks them against the township's zoning rules and master plan. After a short September meeting with no applications, October's agenda had three, all represented by professionals who testified under oath and answered questions from the board's own engineer and planner. Two were approved and one was carried to December. Here's what happened, in the order the board heard them.

1. A flag lot on Old Texas Road is split for two homes, with conditions for the neighbors

Pushtel Construction, Inc. (application PB-1278-26) asked to subdivide a 3.42-acre lot on Old Texas Road (Block 169, Lot 6.08, in the R-30 residential zone) into two lots for two single-family homes. The property is a "flag lot": it has only about 114 feet of road frontage, then widens out behind neighboring homes. It is mostly wooded, with a house its engineer said has been abandoned for years. Splitting the frontage leaves each new lot about 57 feet wide at the road, where the zone requires 150 feet, so the builder needed variances (permission to depart from a zoning rule) for lot frontage and lot width on each lot, plus a "design exception" because one new property line can't run at a right angle to the street. Otherwise, the applicant's engineer and planner testified, both lots far exceed the rules: about 57,000 and 84,000 square feet where 30,000 is required, roughly 130 feet wide where the houses would sit, with each home well over 200 feet back from the road and on its own driveway. The planner argued the odd shape of the existing lot is a hardship that justifies the variances and that the plan does no harm to the neighborhood.

Neighbors pushed back. The couple next door, on Lot 6.07, said they have lived there since 2009 for the privacy, and that the new driveways and a house would sit along their side and back yards. They asked that the rear house be pushed back as far as the existing structure, for buffering, and for removal of two utility poles and a leaning tree near their line. Three residents of River Road, whose homes back onto the property and who said they bought them from the same builder, asked the board to deny the application or, failing that, to bar any further subdivision, keep the access strip as two driveways that never become a road, and fix the homes' locations. One cited a 2020 denial of a different flag-lot application; she was told from the dais that it had no relevance to this application. The applicant's attorney asked one objector about a past warranty dispute with the builder, which she said she had chosen not to pursue.

The board's planner pointed out that a site this size could likely fit four lots if a road were built through it. The applicant agreed that the two lots as designed would effectively prevent that, and agreed on the record to a series of conditions. The board approved the subdivision; every vote the captions caught was yes. The conditions include:

  • No further subdivision of the two lots.
  • The house on the rear lot (6.16) moves back to at least 100 feet from the neighbors' property line, with an evergreen screen and a solid fence. A 15-foot conservation easement, an L-shaped strip controlled by the township, runs along the neighbors' side and rear.
  • Both utility poles on the property come down. The builder will coordinate removal of the leaning tree with the owner and the township.
  • Driveway lighting may not shine onto neighboring properties. Each driveway gets a turnaround so cars don't have to back out.
  • Curb and sidewalk along the property's own frontage. If utility work disturbs the recently paved Old Texas Road, the road gets milled and repaved curb to curb.
  • On-site drainage measures, such as dry wells or yard inlets, so stormwater from the rear lot isn't sent toward existing homes. The builder must also verify there are no wetlands.
  • An overlap in the property descriptions along the neighbors' line gets resolved with new deeds, in the neighbors' favor, before the subdivision is filed.

The houses shown on the plans are placeholders, roughly 2,400-square-foot footprints. The applicant put the total size of each home at roughly 3,800 square feet, and the actual houses will go through the township's building review. The neighbors also asked that their mailing address not change; officials said it would not be affected.

2. Rooftop community solar for a South Middlesex Avenue warehouse is approved

Solar Landscape, LLC (PB-1282-26) won approval to put 3,654 solar panels on the roof of an existing warehouse at 4 South Middlesex Avenue (Block 58, Lot 25), about 14 acres in the light-industrial zone. Its engineer said the panels would produce about 2.1 million kilowatt-hours a year, enough for roughly 330 homes. The panels lie nearly flat, adding about 5 inches to the 26-foot building, and a 6.5-foot inverter brings the top to about 34.5 feet, under the 45-foot limit. On the ground, the project adds about 150 square feet of equipment near the rear of the property, coordinated with JCP&L: a transformer and switchboard protected by bollards, and new 45-foot utility poles. The company's lawyer said a state law signed this fall allows rooftop solar on existing commercial and industrial buildings even where local zoning doesn't list it.

It is a "community solar" project: the power feeds the grid rather than the building, and households and businesses can subscribe for a discount on their electric bills. A company representative said at least 51% of each system's capacity must go to low- and moderate-income subscribers, that subscribers are assigned first come, first served to whichever system has room (so a Monroe subscriber may not be on this one), and that panels last about 25 years, with a decommissioning plan and six months to remove them at the end. The company leases the roof from the building's owner. Answering a resident's questions about how Monroe residents can benefit, Councilman Markel said he is signed up himself and that the township negotiated a 26% discount with Solar Landscape that has been available to residents for over a year. The company said its community-engagement team can work with the township on outreach.

The company asked for one variance, for equipment and overhead lines in a required yard. The board's planner said he didn't think a variance was actually needed but heard the justification anyway. The board's engineer asked for documentation of a railroad-and-utility easement the new poles would cross, as a condition of approval. The approval, with that variance, carried with every vote the captions caught as yes.

3. A drug maker's underground solvent tanks are carried to December

BioZen Therapeutics, Inc. (PB-1273-26) asked for site-plan approval to install four underground tanks, each holding 7,000 liters, at its building at 22 Abeel Road (Block 55, Lot 5.1) in the light-industrial zone. A company engineering manager said the tanks would hold acetone and isopropyl alcohol used to make a pancreatic enzyme product. One tank would hold fresh solvent and the others would handle used solvent that is distilled and reused. Tanker deliveries would come every 15 to 20 days. The site does only research and development now; once production starts, he said, 12 people would work in three shifts. No variances were needed, the tank area adds only about 800 square feet, and the company has an approval letter from the fire official dated September 9.

The board's engineer pressed on what would sit above the tanks and how a tanker would connect to fill them. The testimony and the plans didn't match: the applicant first described gravel or grass, while a detail on its architectural drawings showed a solid masonry enclosure. The board's professionals said they had never received those architectural plans, because the company had switched from above-ground to underground tanks along the way. The project also still needs review by the Environmental Commission, and the board's engineer asked about the depth of the water table; the applicant said it would do soil borings and anchor the tanks if needed. After a five-minute break, the chairman carried the application to the board's December 3 meeting, with no further notice required, so the professionals can review the missing material.

Coming up

The board has no November meeting. Its next meeting is Thursday, December 3, 2026, at 6:30 p.m., when the BioZen application returns.

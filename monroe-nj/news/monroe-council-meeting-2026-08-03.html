Town Council · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's August 3 council meeting

Data centers effectively banned, the old landfill on its way to becoming a solar farm, $4.4 million in water and sewer borrowing approved while a $2.4 million general bond failed, three new police officers, and everything else from a meeting that ran two and a half hours.

Aug 3, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel · 8 min read

Correction (Oct. 15, 2026): An earlier version of this story said the council approved nearly $7 million in new borrowing. That was wrong. The council approved the roughly $4.4 million water-and-sewer bond ordinance, but the roughly $2.4 million general capital bond ordinance failed: it got three yes votes, and the council's attorney noted that bond ordinances need four. We have fixed section 3 below. We also corrected the spelling of several council members' names, which had been taken from automatic captions rather than the township's official roster. The $2.4 million ordinance later came back; see our September 2 and October 5 recaps.

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, August 3, 2026, 6:39–8:56 p.m.
Who was there
Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dipierro, Council Vice President Michael Markel, Council President Rupa Siegel. Councilman Terence Van Dzura was absent.

If you've never sat through a town council meeting, here's the basic shape of one: elected council members vote on permanent local laws, called ordinances, and on smaller one-time official decisions, called resolutions. Before most votes, anyone in the room gets a few minutes to speak. Monroe's August 3 meeting ran two and a half hours and touched more than a dozen separate items — here's everything that happened, biggest stories first.

1. A new rule: no data centers in Monroe, at least for now

A data center is a large building full of computer servers — the machines that power things like artificial intelligence tools and cloud storage. Running and cooling all those servers takes enormous amounts of electricity and water, and as AI companies expand, they're increasingly asking towns like Monroe to host them. The council passed an ordinance this week making clear that data centers are not an allowed use of land anywhere in town, closing a loophole in decades-old zoning language that didn't clearly address them one way or the other. Township officials said the point isn't to rule out data centers forever, but to buy time to write real rules before any company can propose one.

Public comment on this ran long. Several residents connected data centers directly to their own rising electric bills — one woman told the council her bill jumped roughly 20% over the past year, and that she'd had to go without heat some nights this past winter because she couldn't afford to run it. Others raised concerns about New Jersey's ongoing drought and the large volumes of water data centers use for cooling. A few residents pushed back a little on the alarm, arguing that AI-driven infrastructure is coming whether Monroe hosts it or not, and that trying to block it outright — rather than regulate it carefully — could mean missing out on jobs and tax revenue. Township officials said Monroe currently has no plans to offer data center operators any tax or utility discounts, which is something some other towns have controversially done.

2. The town's old landfill is becoming a solar farm

Monroe used to run its own landfill — essentially the town dump — on about 80 acres, until it closed sometime around the 1980s. Because of contamination, it was designated a federal Superfund site, meaning the federal Environmental Protection Agency had to sign off on who was responsible for managing it going forward, a responsibility that fell to a waste company (later absorbed into a larger corporation) the township eventually sued. The council adopted a redevelopment plan this week that rezones the site to allow only one new use: a large community solar array, with the land transferred to a solar company as part of settling that lawsuit. A community solar project lets residents "subscribe" to a share of the farm's output and get a credit on their own electric bill without installing panels on their own roof — and officials confirmed joining wouldn't stop anyone from also putting solar on their own home later. The project still needs a full site plan approved by the planning board before construction happens.

Not everyone was reassured. One resident who says he was a Monroe police officer for 33 years told the council he personally witnessed tanker trucks dumping chemicals at the landfill decades ago, and that he believes it's connected to cancer cases in the surrounding neighborhood; he asked the town to test the ground before installing any solar equipment. That account is one person's decades-old recollection, not an independently confirmed finding, and no evidence was presented at the meeting to verify it either way. Township officials did confirm that a permanent pump system has been removing contaminated water (called "leachate," the liquid that seeps through a closed landfill) from the site for years and will keep doing so indefinitely, regardless of who owns or operates the property.

3. $4.4 million in water and sewer borrowing passes; a $2.4 million general bond fails

Council voted on two bond ordinances — essentially the town taking out a loan, to be paid back over time, which is a standard way local governments pay for large projects instead of raising taxes all at once. The one that passed authorizes about $4.4 million ($4,368,200) in borrowing for the township's water and sewer utility, at an interest rate officials pegged around 3.5%. Officials said it pays for work including refurbishing the Applegarth water tank and relocating a water main on Spotswood-Englishtown Road for the county's culvert project (see below). It passed with all four members present voting yes, which mattered, because the council's attorney noted that bond ordinances require four yes votes.

The second ordinance — about $2.4 million for general capital projects, including the Heritage Chase road paving (which has a $415,000 state grant attached), police equipment and drainage work — did not clear that bar. It drew three yes votes and one no, from Councilman Dipierro, with Councilman Van Dzura absent, and the attorney pointed out partway through the next hearing that it had therefore failed. Before the vote, one resident, pointing to a history of "listed" projects that took years to actually happen, pressed officials on whether the paving and equipment would really get done. Township staff explained that a bond ordinance only authorizes the town to borrow the money — each individual project still has to come back to the council separately for its own approval before anything is spent. Another speaker asked why the town wouldn't pay for smaller items out of its cash reserves; officials said they want to keep a healthy surplus, partly because the township collects taxes on behalf of the school district, fire districts and county. The $2.4 million package returned at the September meeting.

Also from Monday's meeting

Three new police officers sworn in

Before any votes, Police Chief Banos swore in three new officers — Ryan Risco, a former professional cage fighter and Middlesex County corrections officer; Riley Oille, a state wrestling finalist; and Cameron O'Neal — all recent graduates of their police academies, now assigned to patrol, with family members on hand to hold the Bible for each oath.

Nearly $1 million to move a water main for a county flood project, and $93,000 in tax refunds

Council approved a $937,650 contract to relocate a township water main on Spotswood-Englishtown Road so Middlesex County can install new drainage culverts there for flood control; a resident questioned whether the real driver was nearby development rather than flooding. Separately, council approved refunding roughly $93,000 in overpaid property taxes, spread across 14 to 16 different property owners, after the town's tax assessor identified the overpayments.

Solar canopies, fireworks, and National Night Out

The township's administrator reported that solar-canopy construction is moving quickly: about 75% of the panels are installed at the EMS building, and the library's carport will begin construction once the town's farmers market wraps up in late September. Separately, the town's America's 250th fireworks celebration — postponed once already by weather — was rescheduled to Friday, August 28, and National Night Out, the annual community-police event, was set for August 4 at Thompson Park.

Roads, Lynx Drive, and new pickleball courts

The township engineer reported six of about eighteen planned roads repaved so far this year, with weather slowing the rest; milling, paving and curb work on Lynx Drive is complete, helped by a $552,000 state grant; and the new pickleball courts and parking lot at the senior center are essentially finished and — despite some confusion caused by a front-desk miscommunication — open to the public on weekends.

Recurring frustration with the power company, and a tree-permit reminder

More than one council member and resident vented about the regional utility company, JCP&L, over delays moving utility poles and fixing streetlights that have held up road projects at intersections including Applegarth and Prospect Plains; council members also renewed a push to better publicize that residents need a town permit before cutting down a tree, after several people said they didn't know that rule existed, and flagged dead tree limbs hanging over multiple roads as a hazard worth addressing before the next storm.

A crumbling historic barn, and a scam warning for seniors

In a second round of public comment, a resident asked the town to repair the collapsing roof of a historic storage barn near Mounts Mill and Buckelew roads before it's lost entirely; the township's engineer said the structure itself is sound but a leaning addition next to it needs to come down first. Another resident warned fellow seniors about deceptive "your warranty is expiring" mail solicitations circulating locally, urging neighbors not to respond to them.

A library art exhibit, and the 25th anniversary of 9/11

Councilwoman Cohen pointed residents to a current exhibit at the library by a century-old organization of women artists, running for a few more weeks, and reminded the town that this September marks the 25th anniversary of September 11 — Monroe's annual remembrance ceremony will be held as usual at Memorial Tree Park on Prospect Plains Road.

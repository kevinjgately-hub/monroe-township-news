Board of Education · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's October 14 Board of Education meeting

The board president is pressed to apologize for remarks about New Brunswick families, the board invokes the doctrine of necessity four times and votes down five legal checks, and the business administrator explains why Monroe gets no equalization aid.

Oct 14, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the school district's YouTube channel · 10 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Board of Education, regular monthly public meeting (after a closed session)
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Wednesday, October 14, 2026; the public recording runs about 1 hour 52 minutes
Who was there
Board President Gail DiPane, who ran the meeting, Vice President Carmen Alvarez, and members Vanessa Forst, Eric Marozine, Peter Piro, Kate Rattner, Usha Sharma, Peter Tufano and Charles Zeichner, plus Jamesburg representative Dr. Amanda Peck. Acting Superintendent Dr. Adam Layman, Business Administrator and Board Secretary Laura Allen and board attorney Adam Weiss also took part.

The board met in closed session first, to discuss litigation, personnel matters and a harassment, intimidation and bullying (HIB) investigation involving students, then returned for the public meeting. The night combined a detailed lesson in school funding with some of the sharpest exchanges we have covered. Superintendent Dr. Chari Chanley remains on paid leave (see our September 16 recap). Here is everything that happened, biggest stories first.

1. The board president is pressed to apologize for remarks about New Brunswick families

Ms. DiPane opened the public meeting with a statement. She said that at a committee meeting the week before, she made what she called a factual statement about an attendance problem in New Brunswick, one she said affects other districts too, and that she was now being called a racist on social media. She said her statement and intent were not racist and that the people making the accusations were "using race as a means of damaging my reputation just to win votes."

Near the end of the meeting, Mr. Marozine read a prepared statement saying he was "deeply disturbed" by the remarks. He quoted Ms. DiPane as having said at the committee meeting that her daughter teaches in New Brunswick, that families there "don't understand how important it is to be in school," that a child had answered for an absent classmate that he was "still in the DR" (the Dominican Republic), and that this is "the way the children are … brought up." He said the remarks amounted to an unfair generalization that reinforced negative stereotypes about Hispanic families, said her intent couldn't be established from the statement alone, and asked her to apologize publicly to the families of New Brunswick.

Ms. Alvarez said she was at the committee meeting and, as a Hispanic woman, told Ms. DiPane at the time that the comments needed to stop. She said she preferred to handle it privately rather than as "a big spectacle at a board meeting," and that after their conversations Ms. DiPane understood why the comments were insensitive, had been talking about attendance and did not mean them that way. Mr. Tufano asked who Ms. DiPane meant by people "looking for votes"; Ms. DiPane said she had not apologized in her opening statement, and when asked whether she would, said, "I don't need to apologize."

Two residents then spoke. One, who said she was not present for the opening statement, confirmed with Ms. DiPane that she had apologized privately to Ms. Alvarez but not publicly, and thanked Mr. Marozine for raising it. The other, who described herself as a native Spanish speaker whose family traveled abroad every summer, said the message was wrong and urged an apology, and said Ms. DiPane had made comments about special-education students while running for the board; Ms. DiPane said she did not remember it that way. In the end Ms. DiPane said her words "did not come out the right way," that it was a mistake, and that she apologized "to the people that felt insulted" and "for what came out that evening," adding that her words were misinterpreted. We report these statements as they were made and take no position on them.

2. Four "doctrine of necessity" votes, and five legal checks voted down

Mr. Weiss read four resolutions invoking the doctrine of necessity, which under the district's policy lets conflicted members vote when too many members have conflicts of interest for the board to act otherwise (our September recap explains it in more detail). As read, they covered:

  • Paying the law firm Schenck, Price, Smith & King, which the board hired to handle matters related to Dr. Chanley's employment. The resolution listed seven conflicted members. Ms. Alvarez, Ms. DiPane and Ms. Forst are involved in litigation filed by Dr. Chanley. Mr. Piro, Ms. Sharma and Mr. Zeichner were involved in litigation with her. One member's spouse is a district teacher.
  • Votes on matters concerning Dr. Chanley's employment, with the same seven conflicts.
  • Paying Methfessel & Werbel, a firm the board's insurer, the New Jersey Schools Insurance Group, assigned to defend board members. Four members are represented by the firm, and Mr. Marozine has filed litigation in which it is the assigned defense counsel.
  • Paying Flanagan, Barone & O'Brien, another insurer-assigned defense firm. Six members were or are represented by it in litigation filed by Dr. Chanley.

All four passed. Mr. Tufano voted no on each, and the captions don't record a vote from Ms. Rattner on any of them.

The disagreement showed up in the vote on the monthly bills. Before voting, Mr. Tufano asked Mr. Weiss whether board members are obligated to pay contractual legal bills for employees; Mr. Weiss answered, "To the extent that there is a contract for services, yes." Mr. Tufano then voted yes on everything. But a majority of members voted no on checks numbered 17670 through 17673 and check 19531, and Ms. Allen announced that those five checks did not pass while everything else did. As the captions record it, Ms. Alvarez, Mr. Piro, Ms. Sharma and Ms. DiPane voted no on them, Ms. Forst and Ms. Rattner abstained on the first four, and Mr. Zeichner's vote on them is garbled. The recording doesn't say what the checks were for. At the board's November 9 special meeting, Mr. Tufano said the board majority had repeatedly ignored advice that it is contractually obligated to pay Dr. Chanley's legal bills.

3. Why Monroe gets no "equalization aid"

At the finance committee's request, Ms. Allen walked the full board through how the state calculated Monroe's 2026–27 state aid. She said the state Department of Education is gathering input on changing the school-funding formula, but the district has been told not to expect significant changes until after the 2027–28 budget year, so it is planning under the current formula. Highlights:

  • Enrollment drives it. The state projected Monroe's resident enrollment for October 15, 2026, at about 5,992 students, down from a high of 6,729 in October 2020. The official count was taken the day after the meeting. Full-day kindergartners now count as full students instead of halves, which Ms. Allen estimated would add only about $15,000 to $20,000.
  • No equalization aid. The formula sets an "adequacy budget" — what the state says it should cost to educate Monroe's students — at about $120 million, and a "local fair share" the community can afford, based on property values and residents' income, at about $192 million. Because the local share is higher, Monroe gets no equalization aid, which she said has been true for years.
  • Special education. The state counts two-thirds of special-education costs in the adequacy budget and pays the other third as categorical aid, about $7 million. Ms. Allen said that if the split were reversed, Monroe would get about $14 million.
  • Other aid. Transportation aid is about $3.8 million and security aid about $983,000. Total general-fund state aid is about $11.9 million. The state capped how much any district could lose at 3%, which Ms. Allen put at about $369,000 less than last year for Monroe. Aid per pupil was $1,992, versus $1,995 last year and $1,435 in 2022–23.

One board member said the state was taking money from a district that already gets no equalization aid while health and energy costs soar, and criticized State Sen. Linda Greenstein by name for not coming to the board to explain why she isn't fighting for more aid. Ms. Rattner said all three of the district's state legislators should be asked to advocate, and that she is hopeful the state's new education commissioner and administration will change the formula. She also questioned why only about 500 regular-education students and 131 special-education students count for transportation aid when the district buses most students; Ms. Allen and Dr. Layman said the count reflects only students actually assigned to a bus on October 15, and that walkers and Jamesburg students, whose busing Jamesburg pays for, aren't included.

Also from Wednesday's meeting

A new on-demand mental-health program

The board approved a one-year, $32,500 agreement with Insight Health and Insight Medical PA. Dr. Layman said it gives high-risk students quick access to a psychologist for evaluation, instead of waiting on outside contractors, hospitals or crisis centers, where he said a student can sometimes wait an entire weekend. A resident who supported the idea asked how the student data in its software would be protected. Dr. Layman said it syncs with the district's Genesis student system, that access starts with case managers and guidance counselors under building administrators' oversight and follows the district's student-data access protocol, and that records are kept as student records. He said the platform could later replace the district's current anti-bullying (HIB) tracking software and adds a mental-health threat-assessment tool.

Attendance, a new college-credit course and other committee reports

Mr. Zeichner reported that the curriculum committee heard that Woodland School, the middle school and the high school will be on a state-required corrective plan for chronic absenteeism, which includes meeting with parents. It also reviewed a new honors college entrepreneurship course for grades 10–12 that carries University of Delaware credit for students who earn at least a C, with a $500 course fee, and a new kindergarten report card. Ms. Alvarez reported that the district has kept up planned replacement of its 42 roof sections rather than letting repairs pile up, that the first school solar installations were expected to start generating electricity in October with savings toward the end of 2026, that the Honeywell HVAC project is largely on schedule, and that the district has enough bus drivers to cover most absences. Ms. Rattner said the policy committee recommended abolishing two policies, on missing children and drug-free school zones, that are already covered elsewhere, and that applications for the board's citizens advisory committee, now open to students too, would go out within weeks.

Personnel, donations and an e-rate consultant

The board approved its personnel lists, including five retirements. Dr. Layman said a temporary increase in kindergarten aides' hours covered only September, to help with the switch to full-day kindergarten. Board members thanked the Friends of the Monroe Township Community Garden for a $500 donation to the high school's transition-to-adult program, along with donations from the American Heart Association and a resident who gave a piano to Woodland School. Answering Mr. Marozine, Ms. Allen said the consultant that applies for federal E-rate technology discounts is paid 15% of whatever funding it brings in, with no cap, so the district pays nothing if nothing comes in.

Student reports and announcements

Student board members reported on Week of Respect activities at every school, fire-safety assemblies, the high school's college fair with more than 75 colleges, a marching band home show with visiting bands, and the drama club's fall musical. They also said the cellphone rule continues to work well. Dr. Layman congratulated the band and fall athletes. The next regular meeting was set for November 18 at 6:15 p.m.

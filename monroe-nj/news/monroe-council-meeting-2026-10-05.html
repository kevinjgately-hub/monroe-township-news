Town Council · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's October 5 council meeting

The old landfill's sale to a solar developer is approved after a pointed debate over who pays, a $1.88 million bond finally passes, a study of four Applegarth Road lots raises questions, and the township gets a clean audit.

Oct 5, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel · 10 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, October 5, 2026, starting at 6:30 p.m.; the recording runs about 1 hour 52 minutes
Who was there
All five council members — Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dipierro, Councilman Terence Van Dzura, Council Vice President Michael Markel and Council President Rupa Siegel — plus Mayor Stephen Dalina and Business Administrator Kevin McGowan.

The meeting opened with a moment of silence for John Riggs, a longtime Monroe public servant who died September 21 in Arizona. In the course of his career he served on the zoning and planning boards, on the council, and as the township's director of planning and zoning and director of environmental protection, and council members and residents returned to his memory all night. Most of the votes were second readings — the public hearing and final vote on ordinances (permanent local laws) that were introduced at the September 2 meeting. Here's everything that happened, biggest stories first.

1. The old landfill is sold to a solar company, with one council member abstaining

Council gave final approval to ordinance O-9-2026-018, which authorizes the township to sell its former landfill — about 80 acres — and to enter a redevelopment agreement with CEP Renewables, LLC, along with neighboring parcels owned by BFI Waste Systems of New Jersey. The vote was four yes and one abstention, from Councilman Dipierro. The deal grows out of the settlement of the township's lawsuit against BFI: officials said the council voted to settle about a year ago, that a federal judge oversees the settlement and receives a progress report about every 60 days, and that by handing the landfill off, the township stops paying the site's ongoing costs and sheds the long-term liability for it. The redevelopment plan limits the site to solar (see our August 3 recap), and the company must still win site-plan approval from the Planning Board. Officials said the project would be a "community solar" farm, where residents subscribe to a share of the output for a discount on their electric bill; the few streets of the Inwood Estates neighborhood next to the landfill would get first chance to sign up, with leftover capacity opened to the rest of Monroe and then beyond.

The sharpest exchange was over money. Several residents, and Councilman Dipierro, said the public was repeatedly told the township would be made whole for the money it spent on the landfill's cleanup. Officials said the township recovers its roughly $2.5 million investment in the pipeline and pump station that carry the landfill's leachate (contaminated liquid that seeps through a closed landfill) to the county utilities authority, which treats it, through the sewer rate the landfill's owner pays — an industrial rate they described as $12 per 1,000 gallons plus a minimum quarterly charge, bringing in roughly $125,000 to $150,000 a year — and that the bond for that work runs 30 years. Dipierro, who lives near the landfill, argued the township should more than triple that industrial rate, said the township had already spent more than $600,000 on engineering studies, and said the landfill is only about 25% capped; he asked the council to table the vote. The township's attorney replied that the settlement is favorable, that the rate can be revisited through a rate study without undoing it, and that dropping the landfill's liability — potentially millions of dollars a year in future costs, he said — was central to the council's decision. Dipierro said he appreciated the explanation and that the township needs to be more transparent about its numbers; the ordinance then passed.

2. The $1.88 million bond ordinance finally passes

After failing in August and passing a first vote in September, the bond ordinance for general capital projects was adopted on October 5. It appropriates $2,392,045 and authorizes $1,882,855 in bonds or notes — the township's borrowing. Councilman Dipierro voted no. The captions don't clearly pick up every council member's vote, but because bond ordinances need four yes votes, its adoption means the tally was 4–1. Officials listed what it pays for: the Heritage Chase road repaving, where a $415,000 state grant covers part of the cost and the borrowing covers about $1.3 million of the rest; police cameras, radios, body armor and vehicles; the township's annual drainage work; HVAC and roof repairs at municipal buildings; and the "remount" of an ambulance — putting the existing ambulance body on a new chassis for about $275,000, versus roughly $450,000 for a new ambulance. Officials said the bonds average about an eight-year term and that the items were all part of the 2026 capital budget.

Residents who spoke wanted to know whether this was the same ordinance as the one from August (officials said yes, essentially unchanged) and criticized borrowing for relatively low-priced items, saying interest can add up. Administrator McGowan and the council president answered that the township pays for some items from its operating budget and with grants and borrows for others, and that the administration believes funding these items this way is prudent. Our September 2 recap covers the earlier round of debate, including Dipierro's concerns about cash reserves.

3. A study of four lots on Applegarth Road raises questions

Among the routine resolutions approved as a block was R10-2026-173, which asks the Planning Board to study four lots on Applegarth Road, across from the Applegarth firehouse near the Gateway community, to see whether they qualify as an "area in need of redevelopment." Officials stressed that this is a "non-condemnation" study, meaning the township would never take private property, and that it is only an analysis by the board's planner of whether the lots meet one or more of 12 legal criteria. Two of the lots are owned by the township (roughly 11 acres, as officials described it), and two are privately owned (roughly six to seven acres). If they qualify, the township could then draft a redevelopment plan and decide whether to bring in a developer; officials said that decision, and any discussion of uses, comes later. A water tank sits on one lot and would be carved out into its own lot if anything were ever built.

Two residents pressed on why the township wanted the study. Officials said the idea came from the township's master plan, which calls for identifying run-down or underused parcels, that no developer had approached the township, and that their preliminary thinking is light commercial use that nearby residents could use — not housing and not heavy commercial or warehousing. One resident urged that the land stay as passive open space to soak up rainwater; officials said "everything's on the table" and that a Planning Board hearing and recommendation to the council would come first, with more chances for public comment.

Also from Monday's meeting

A clean audit, and how close the town is to its borrowing limit

The township's outside auditor told the council that the 2025 audit received an unmodified ("clean") opinion, with no findings of non-compliance on more than $5.5 million in state grants and no significant deficiencies or material weaknesses in internal controls, and no audit recommendations — the finance chief's first full year in the job. According to the auditor, New Jersey towns may borrow up to 3.5% of their average property value; he put Monroe's limit at about $525 million, of which the town has used 15.1%, leaving roughly $446 million of headroom. Several council members praised the finance staff. Separately, the administrator reported that the township refunded $371,945.99 in 2025 as a result of tax-appeal settlements, covering 23 properties, answering a question from the September meeting.

The police station: a new contractor, and a target of spring

Asked about the unfinished police station, the council president said the surety bond company — the insurer that guarantees a construction contractor's work — has hired a general contractor that started a couple of weeks ago, has a schedule it is keeping, and has crews on site. Officials said a substantial amount of the work should be done by the end of the year, with punch-list items finishing in March or April, and that no cost overruns are projected; if that changes, the council would have to vote on it.

The political-signs rule is adopted

Council adopted, 5–0, ordinance O-9-2026-019, which lets campaign signs go up earlier before an election. Under it, signs may be placed beginning 45 days before the date state law requires ballots to be mailed — about 90 days before Election Day — and must come down within 10 days after. Officials said that, by tying the rule to the state's ballot-mailing date, the ordinance wouldn't need to be amended again if the state changes that date. One resident called 90 days too long and said it should be debated further. Officials confirmed signs remain banned on trees, fences, utility poles, light poles and public property, and that zoning officers remove them and notify the campaign's designated contact so the sign can be picked up from the public works yard.

Housing liens released, and a canceled truck purchase

Council released two liens tied to affordable-housing ownership units — $54,325 and $79,356 — because the homes were sold, and canceled a resolution from last year to buy a 2025 Ford vehicle. A separate resolution, R10-2026-186, was pulled from the block for its own vote and passed, with Councilman Dipierro abstaining; the recording doesn't say what it covered. Dipierro also abstained on the monthly bill-payment vote.

Streetlights, roads, and the Applegarth–Prospect Plains intersection

The township engineer said one road remains in this year's paving program, along with guide-rail work on Spotswood-Gravel Hill Road, that the James Monroe Park concession stand is in its final punch-list stage, and that the senior-center pickleball courts are open with landscaping left. Councilman Dipierro said work at Applegarth and Prospect Plains Roads has seemed idle for weeks and raised numerous out streetlights in his ward; Administrator McGowan said the electric utility, JCP&L, made progress earlier this year but response times have slipped again, that the township's staff and police have been reporting outages themselves, and called the situation "unacceptable." A resident also raised the stalled intersection work.

A state report on surplus land

Dipierro asked about a recent state report on surplus state-owned land that could be used for affordable housing. McGowan said the report identifies mostly NJ Transit and state transportation property, that much of the state land in Monroe is protected by open-space or farmland designations, and that nobody from the state has approached the township about using land here. The township attorney added that the initial focus is roughly 800 acres of NJ Transit land.

Legal bills and insurance, explained

Responding to Dipierro, the township attorney said that claims covered by the township's insurance pool are paid by the pool, but "prerogative writ" cases — lawsuits challenging a zoning or land-use decision — are not insurance claims and are paid by the township. He added that a council member sued over official duties would generally be covered by the pool, while a personal matter like a car accident would not.

EMS numbers, electric-vehicle chargers and the Thompson Park paths

Council President Siegel, citing posts on the township EMS department's Facebook page, said September saw 941 calls for service (852 EMS and 89 fire), including 59 motor-vehicle crashes, with Wednesdays the busiest day, and an average EMS response time of about 5.6 minutes versus a regional benchmark of 90% within 10 minutes. She also noted new power stretchers and new walking paths at Thompson Park. In answer to a question from Dipierro, McGowan said the township's electric-vehicle charging stations bring in modest revenue — the township charges $1.50 for the first hour and $5 afterward — and that it roughly covers their electricity cost.

What residents raised in public comment

Beyond the items above, two residents used public comment to criticize the Board of Education, including its handling of the superintendent's contract and legal costs; those remarks were their own claims, which we have not verified, and the school board is a separate body from the council (see our August 19 Board of Education recap). A resident again asked the council to consider a resolution declaring Monroe a sanctuary city, noting it "didn't make the cut" for the agenda; there was no council response on the record. Another resident asked the township to press utilities to mow under high-tension power lines later in the season to reduce fall pollen, and asked about the stalled intersection work noted above.

Coming up in Monroe

Officials highlighted: the Oktoberfest event on October 18; a county-sponsored "Business Resources for Success" workshop for small businesses on October 27 from 9 to 11:30 a.m. at the Community Center; a family fun night at a local farm on October 28 at 4 p.m.; and the township's Diwali celebration on Sunday, November 1, from 4 to 7 p.m. at the senior center. Councilwoman Cohen reminded residents that mail-in ballots are out and early voting is coming, and that the township's Health Council now has a Facebook page. Council members also welcomed two new businesses, a hair salon and a paint-and-sip art studio, and the mayor thanked the 11 restaurants that donated food to the Taste of Monroe food-pantry event, which Vice President Markel said drew nearly 300 people.

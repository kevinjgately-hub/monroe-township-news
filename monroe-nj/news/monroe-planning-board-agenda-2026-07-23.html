Planning Board · Regular Meeting

Everything that happened at Monroe's July 23 Planning Board meeting

A zoning tweak that's really about heading off data centers, the old landfill's solar redevelopment plan clears a step with new answers about who's on the hook financially, and a wooded subdivision's paperwork finally gets sorted out.

Meeting held Tuesday, July 23, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording · 5 min read

Update: This story originally ran as an agenda-only preview, because no usable video transcript or minutes were available at the time. The township has since posted a working recording of this meeting, so this is now a full recap based on what was actually said and decided.

What: Monroe Township Planning Board, regular monthly meeting

Where: Monroe Township Municipal Building

When: Tuesday, July 23, 2026, 6:30 p.m.

Who was there: Chairman Marc Gaffrey presiding, with most of the board present; Manish Patel and Jay Weiner did not answer the roll call

No new applications were scheduled, so the board spent its evening on two items sent up from the town council for a "consistency review" — a required step where the Planning Board checks whether a proposed zoning ordinance lines up with the township's long-term master plan before the council can formally adopt it — plus routine paperwork. Both ordinances discussed here went before the town council for final adoption at its August 3 meeting.

A zoning tweak was really about getting ahead of data centers

Township Business Administrator Kevin McGowan told the board that ordinance O-6-2026-013 exists because of a wave of resident questions about data centers — the large, power-hungry server warehouses that have become a flashpoint in towns across New Jersey. No company has proposed building one in Monroe, and data centers aren't a permitted use anywhere in the township, McGowan said. But four of the township's zoning districts — Planned Office Commercial, Highway Development, Light Industrial, and Office Professional — still contain decades-old language permitting "data processing facilities," vague enough that officials worried it could someday be stretched to cover something like a modern data center. The ordinance simply deletes that phrase from all four districts, closing the loophole before anyone tries to use it.

The board's professional planner told the board that large-scale data centers' heavy water and electricity use would conflict with the master plan's environmental and conservation goals, even though the 2022 master plan doesn't mention data centers directly — the technology's local controversy is barely a year old. One resident asked detailed questions about other, unrelated uses listed in the same section of the ordinance, such as "office research" and a "research laboratory" use; board members clarified that those were pre-existing permitted uses, unrelated to data centers, whose numbering just shifted because of the deleted text. The board voted unanimously to recommend the ordinance as consistent with the master plan.

The old landfill's solar redevelopment plan moved forward — with new answers about who pays

The board also reviewed ordinance O-6-2026-014, the formal redevelopment plan for the township's roughly 85-acre former municipal landfill off Spotswood-Gravel Hill and Lanny roads — a closed federal Superfund site the board had earlier this year recommended designating an "area in need of redevelopment." The plan sets up overlay zoning that allows only one use on the site: a utility-scale solar array feeding power into the electric grid, complete with required buffers, height limits, landscaping, and — unusually for a zoning ordinance — a mandatory decommissioning plan spelling out what happens to the equipment once its useful life ends. Township Business Administrator Kevin McGowan and the board's planner said the project is expected to be a "community solar" array, meaning a share of the power gets sold to local residents, particularly in the neighboring Inwood Estates section, at a discounted rate. The board found it consistent with the master plan's renewable-energy goals and voted unanimously to recommend it.

During public comment, a resident pressed on a longstanding worry: whether Monroe taxpayers still owe money tied to the old landfill's environmental cleanup. The township's attorney and Business Administrator McGowan explained that a settlement agreement with BFI/Republic — the waste-industry company blamed for the original contamination — lays out a specific sequence of steps: the redevelopment-area designation (already done), this redevelopment plan, and then a still-unsigned agreement with the redeveloper, previously reported to be CEP Renewables, which builds solar arrays on other former landfills nationally. Once that agreement is finalized, officials said, CEP takes over ownership and responsibility for the site, and the township's financial exposure ends; the attorney told the resident directly that Monroe should not be on the hook for "large sums of money" once the process concludes. Neither official cited a specific dollar figure or timeline for when that final step would happen.

Also from Tuesday's meeting

A subdivision cleanup drew a question about setting precedent

Greenvest, LLC asked the board to reapprove a minor subdivision it had originally granted in 2023 and affirmed again in 2024, after a legal filing deadline was missed while the property changed hands. The subdivision itself untangles decades of small property-line encroachments — sheds, fences — between a few existing homes on Hoffman Road and a larger wooded parcel that will be placed under a permanent conservation easement and can never be built on. As it did originally, the board waived its standard requirement that the applicant install curbing and sidewalks, since the land in question will never see construction. Vice Chairman David Rothman asked the board's attorney whether routinely waiving that requirement for some applicants but not others could someday expose the board to a legal challenge; the attorney responded that each application is judged on its own documented facts, and this property's permanent conservation restrictions and lack of any future construction gave the board a solid, specific basis for the waiver. The board approved the reaffirmation unanimously.

Two other applications were formally signed off, and the meeting wrapped early

The board adopted written resolutions memorializing two previously-approved applications — Kings Row Developers, LLC and 30 Abeel Road SG, LLC — a routine step documenting decisions the board had already made verbally at earlier meetings, with no new testimony or discussion. With nothing else on the agenda, the meeting adjourned in well under an hour. The board's next regular meeting is scheduled for August 27, 2026.

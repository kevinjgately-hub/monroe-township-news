Town Council · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's September 2 council meeting

The landfill-to-solar sale clears its first vote, a bond ordinance that failed in August comes back and clears a first vote, the town's fight with its old insurance fund comes up in public comment, and a $5.7 million water-treatment contract is approved.

Sep 2, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel · 7 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Wednesday, September 2, 2026, starting at 6:30 p.m.; adjourned about 7:52 p.m.
Who was there
All five council members — Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dipierro, Councilman Terence Van Dzura, Council Vice President Michael Markel and Council President Rupa Siegel — plus Mayor Stephen Dalina and Business Administrator Kevin McGowan.

Many votes in New Jersey towns happen in two steps. An ordinance (a permanent local law) is first introduced at a "first reading" — a preliminary vote — and then comes back at a later meeting for a "second reading," a public hearing and the final vote. Most of the big items on September 2 were first readings, so you'll see several of them again in our October 5 recap. Here's everything that happened, biggest stories first.

1. The old landfill's sale to a solar company takes its first formal step

Council voted to advance ordinance O-9-2026-018 on first reading. It would approve a purchase, sale and redevelopment agreement between the township and CEP Renewables, LLC for the former municipal landfill (tax lots 36.01 and 37 in block 148) and for neighboring parcels owned by BFI Waste Systems of New Jersey, the waste company that the township sued over the landfill (see our August 3 recap). Alongside it, council approved a resolution naming CEP Renewables a "conditional redeveloper," an interim status that lets a company begin working with the township on a project while it covers the township's costs under an interim agreement. Officials said the company must put up a $25,000 escrow — a deposit the township can draw on to pay its own planners and lawyers for reviewing the project. The township's redevelopment plan limits the site to solar, and officials stressed that the company would still have to file a full site plan with the Planning Board before anything gets built.

The most pointed questions were about money and cleanup. Councilman Dipierro asked who pays the bill for treating "leachate," the contaminated liquid that seeps through a closed landfill and is piped to the Middlesex County Utilities Authority for treatment. Officials answered that whoever operates the site pays it — BFI today, and CEP after the deal closes. Some residents were uneasy about the BFI-owned buffer parcels next to the landfill and about who would be liable for any contamination; officials said CEP would own all of the land and would be responsible to the state Department of Environmental Protection. One resident suggested taxpayers would still end up paying the treatment bill; officials said that isn't the case. Others asked why the first chance to subscribe to power from the solar farm would go to a neighborhood next to the site rather than the whole town — a question that came up again at the October meeting.

2. A bond ordinance that failed in August comes back and clears a first vote, 4–1

The roughly $2.4 million capital package that failed on August 3 (it got only three of the four yes votes a bond ordinance needs) returned as a new first-reading ordinance. On the official agenda it appropriates $2,392,045 and authorizes $1,882,855 in bonds or notes — the town's borrowing — to pay part of the cost. It passed 4–1, with Councilman Dipierro voting no. A key piece of the package is repaving in the Heritage Chase neighborhood, which is partly funded by a $415,000 state Department of Transportation grant. The same night, council approved a resolution asking the state for more time to award the construction contract; officials said the contract needs to be awarded by November 13, and officials tied the borrowing to that timeline.

Dipierro said he's worried about the town's dwindling cash reserves and questioned borrowing for items like vehicles on a nine-year bond, saying the town could pay cash for $30,000–$40,000 purchases. Administrator McGowan answered that the package had already been pared down, and that the township needs a healthy cash surplus partly because it collects taxes on behalf of the school district, fire districts and county — money that flows through the township's books on a roughly $100 million annual budget, as he described it. The package came back for its public hearing and final vote on October 5.

3. The township's old insurance fund wants money — and the town is disputing it

During public comment, residents asked about the township's insurance coverage and about legal bills. Officials explained that Monroe left the Middlesex JIF — a "JIF" is a pool of towns that buy liability coverage together — and has been in a different pool, the Garden State JIF, for about five years. As best the captions can be read, the township pays roughly $1.7 million a year in premiums to the current fund, and covers the first $25,000 of each claim itself (called a "self-insured retention") before the fund steps in. Councilman Dipierro added that the fund covers the township attorney's fees for settlements once that deductible is met.

The more unusual piece: officials said the former fund, the Middlesex JIF, has sent the township letters seeking a payment tied to the township's earlier membership, and that the township disputes the demand. They said the township made one payment under protest and has made none since. The dollar amount was not clear in the captions, so we are not stating it here, and the dispute was not on the council's agenda that night.

Also from Wednesday's meeting

A $5.7 million water-treatment contract

Among the routine items approved together as a block (a "consent agenda") was a $5,735,000 contract with Sovereign Consulting, Inc. for PFAS treatment at the township's Well No. 17/19. PFAS are a group of manufactured "forever chemicals" that have turned up in water supplies nationwide and that the EPA now regulates in drinking water. Other items in the same block included $130,392 for four replacement pumps at a utility pump station, an $18,800 state grant for a Green Communities inventory, and the release of a developer's cash performance guarantee after maintenance guarantees were posted; the release was voted on separately from the others but also passed.

Political signs could go up three months before an election

The council advanced an ordinance (O-9-2026-019) that would let campaign signs go up roughly 90 days before an election instead of the current 45, and still requires them to come down within 10 days afterward. Backers tied the change to mail-in ballots, which now go out about 45 days before Election Day. It passed its first reading and came back for a final vote in October.

Roads, traffic, and the Applegarth–Prospect Plains intersection

The township engineer reported that the 2026 paving program is nearly done — about 18 roads milled and paved with one left — and that county work at the Prospect Plains and Applegarth Road intersection has been delayed by waiting on the electric and phone companies to move utility poles, with an overnight closure of northbound Applegarth planned to wrap up the work the following week. Councilman Dipierro mentioned utility-department patch paving at the municipal building and at a fire house, and striping on Federal Road. He also raised traffic on Applegarth Road: officials said there is no county-wide regional traffic study, though the county is upgrading signals at Union Valley, Cranberry Station and Applegarth with left-turn arrows, with costs shared by the county and a developer.

The state's planned juvenile facility

A resident asked about the state's plans for a juvenile detention facility on state-owned land in Monroe. Officials said the state's 2028 target looks unrealistic and that it could take four to six years, that an Attorney General's task force is involved, and that the facility is expected to house up to about 100 residents with roughly 400 employees.

Budget answers for a resident

In response to a resident's questions, officials said the township is analyzing whether to move from fully insured to self-insured health coverage, with results expected in a couple of months; that the capital plan matches the budget; that the latest audit had no material internal-control comments; and that the finance office is working with the auditors to publish interim financial statements on a regular basis. They also said the state's property-tax relief data are expected next year, and that dental coverage for a small number of retirees continues under older employment agreements; Dipierro abstained on the monthly bill-payment vote. Another speaker asked about the township's 2025 tax-appeal settlements; the administrator promised to provide the figure, which he later did in October.

Other public comment

Residents also asked about water and electric bills, why poll-worker training is held only in Piscataway, and why the township's zoning map on file is dated 2017 (officials said it was updated in 2022 alongside the master plan but the hard copy still carries the older date). One resident asked the council to consider a resolution declaring Monroe a sanctuary city at a future meeting; there was no council response on the record. Another speaker paid tribute to a recently deceased longtime Monroe resident who had served on township commissions, and a resident asked the council to remember 9/11 on its 25th anniversary.

Community notices

Councilman Dipierro noted that the township's postponed Fourth of July fireworks had been held on August 28. Councilwoman Cohen highlighted a "Keep Middlesex Moving" presentation on transportation options for older adults. Council Vice President Markel said the senior center logs roughly 85,000 visits a year and announced the Taste of Monroe food-pantry fundraiser on September 23 (6–7:30 p.m.). Mayor Dalina announced the 9/11 remembrance ceremony at 8:46 a.m. on September 11, a yoga and sound-bath event on September 22 and a hike on October 1. Council also issued proclamations for Childhood Cancer Awareness Month and Senior Center Month.

Board of Education · Special Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's November 9 special Board of Education meeting

In a 30-minute special meeting, the board votes 6–2 to invoke the "doctrine of necessity" so conflicted members can vote on recommendations about Superintendent Dr. Chari Chanley's employment, residents urge it to wait for newly elected members, and the board goes behind closed doors.

Nov 9, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the school district's YouTube channel · 5 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Board of Education, special meeting: a public session, then a closed (executive) session
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, November 9, 2026, ahead of a 6:30 p.m. closed session; the public recording runs about 30 minutes
Who was there
Board President Gail DiPane, who ran the meeting, Vice President Carmen Alvarez, and members Vanessa Forst, Eric Marozine, Peter Piro, Usha Sharma, Peter Tufano and Charles Zeichner. Member Kate Rattner and Jamesburg representative Dr. Amanda Peck were not present at roll call. Business Administrator and Board Secretary Laura Allen called the roll, and board attorney Adam Weiss read the resolutions; the board's special counsel also attended.

The board called this special meeting to take up matters involving Superintendent Dr. Chari Chanley, who has been on paid leave while the board pursues an investigation of possible tenure charges against her (see our August 19 and September 16 recaps). Tenure charges are the formal legal process New Jersey requires before a tenured educator can be dismissed. The public part of the meeting lasted about half an hour: a 30-minute public forum, a vote to invoke the "doctrine of necessity," and a vote to go into closed session. Here is what happened.

1. The board invokes the doctrine of necessity again, 6–2

Mr. Weiss read a resolution invoking the doctrine of necessity "with respect to the board's votes related to Dr. Chanley's employment." As we explained in our September 16 recap, New Jersey's School Ethics Act generally bars board members with a conflict of interest from voting on a matter, but if so many members are conflicted that the board can't act, the district's policy lets them vote anyway, provided the board announces it publicly, explains the conflicts, posts the resolution for 30 days and sends a copy to the state School Ethics Commission.

The resolution said the board intended to vote that evening on "recommendations by special counsel relative to the employment of Dr. Chanley." As read, it said Ms. Alvarez, Ms. DiPane and Ms. Forst are involved in litigation filed by Dr. Chanley, that one member's spouse is a teacher employed by the district, and that Dr. Chanley is actively suing the board; because of those conflicts, it said, the board lacks a quorum to vote on personnel matters concerning her. (The September resolution on the same subject listed six conflicted members; the captions of this reading may not capture the full list.) It passed 6–2. Voting yes: Ms. Alvarez, Ms. Forst, Mr. Piro, Ms. Sharma, Mr. Zeichner and Ms. DiPane. Voting no: Mr. Marozine and Mr. Tufano.

Before the vote, Mr. Marozine asked what deadline or consequence made it necessary to act that night, citing a court decision he said holds that conflicts alone don't establish necessity, and whether the doctrine lets conflicted members join closed-session discussions or only vote. Mr. Weiss said the board is within its rights to put an item on its agenda and act on it, that it was his opinion and special counsel's that the doctrine be invoked so the board could discuss and vote on the matter, and that he could not discuss specifics in public but would talk with Mr. Marozine privately. Mr. Marozine noted that in the past 18 months conflicted members had been kept out of closed-session discussions of personnel matters, and said "we just do whatever we want here"; the president called the remark uncalled for.

2. A heated exchange after the vote

After the vote, Mr. Tufano asked the members who voted yes to explain why, saying special counsel had told the board the doctrine wasn't needed. (Mr. Weiss had just said the opposite; we could not resolve the difference from the recording.) The president said she had the right to vote as she chose and that she voted yes so she could take part in the discussion. Ms. Alvarez said she would not disclose privileged attorney-client information. Mr. Zeichner said the board attorney had advised him on the doctrine and that he did not owe an explanation of his vote. Mr. Tufano then told Mr. Weiss that the board majority had repeatedly ignored his advice, including, he said, advice that the board is contractually obligated to pay Dr. Chanley's legal bills, and predicted a major lawsuit whose costs taxpayers would bear. He also said that anyone who discusses the superintendent in the closed session "should be jailed." Members of the audience called out at several points, and the president warned she would stop the meeting if the interruptions continued. We report these statements as they were made and take no position on them.

3. The board goes into closed session

The board then voted 7–1, as the captions record the roll call, to go into closed session to discuss pending or anticipated litigation and matters covered by attorney-client privilege, and matters involving employees and the terms of their employment; only Mr. Marozine voted no. The resolution said action may be taken on those items. Just before the vote, Mr. Tufano asked whether members would apologize to residents if what happens in closed session leads to more lawsuits; the president said it was 6:30 and the board was going into closed session.

The posted recording ends with a motion to adjourn and the president's reminder that the next board meeting was Wednesday, November 18. It does not show the closed session itself, any return to public session, or any public vote on Dr. Chanley's employment, so we cannot report what, if anything, the board decided that night.

What residents said in the public forum

Four people spoke, all about the superintendent's case. One resident urged the board not to file new tenure charges against Dr. Chanley, saying the case has already cost taxpayers heavily and that any further litigation would pull money away from classrooms. He asked that the decision wait until newly elected board members take office, and asked the board to disclose how much it has spent on her salary and benefits during her suspension and on legal fees. A second resident said he was speaking against the board's leadership rather than for or against Dr. Chanley. He argued that the agenda's description of the closed-session topic was legally inadequate and that any action must be voted on in public, demanded the release of the original allegations and investigators' reports and fees, and warned conflicted members they could face ethics complaints. He also said the board had already diverted about $600,000 to legal costs; we have not verified that figure.

Two former board members also spoke. One said that while she served, the School Ethics Commission sent the board a letter saying that even after the doctrine of necessity is invoked, conflicted members may not take part in closed-session discussion of the matter they are conflicted on, and that the doctrine is meant only to keep district operations going. The other said that, as an outgoing member, she had been advised by the board attorney to limit herself to business needed to run the district, and argued that Ms. Alvarez, as an outgoing member, should not take part in decisions reaching beyond her term. These were the speakers' own claims and interpretations; the board did not respond to them on the record.

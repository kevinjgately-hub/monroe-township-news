Town Council · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's April 6 council meeting

The mayor presents a $79.5 million budget with a 4.3-cent tax rate increase, the council adopts a $2.4 million road bond and hears how much the township owes, the regional watershed study gets more expensive, and residents ask for lower gun permit fees and a light pollution law.

Apr 6, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel · 11 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, April 6, 2026; the recording runs about 1 hour 47 minutes, and the agenda session adjourned at 7:11 p.m. before the regular meeting began
Who was there
Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dipierro, Councilman Terence Van Dzura, Council Vice President Michael Markel, Council President Rupa Siegel, Mayor Stephen Dalina and Business Administrator Kevin McGowan. All five council members were present. The meeting opened with a moment of silence for the township's longtime construction official, who had died recently.

Monroe's council meets in two parts on the same night: an agenda session, where the council reads through the items and residents can ask about them, and a regular meeting, where the votes happen. An ordinance is a permanent local law that must be voted on twice: first reading is the introduction, and second reading, after a public hearing, is the vote to adopt. A resolution is a one-time official decision. This meeting had one ordinance up for adoption (a road bond), three introduced (a zoning change, a budget "cap bank" and a fee update, which were adopted on May 4; see our May 4 recap), and the mayor's presentation of the 2026 budget.

1. The mayor and business administrator present the 2026 budget

The proposed 2026 township budget is $79,527,373. It would raise the municipal tax rate by 4.3 cents, to 56.6 cents per $100 of assessed value, which Business Administrator Kevin McGowan said works out to about $148.68 more per year, or about $12 a month, for the average Monroe home, assessed at $338,100. The municipal portion is only about 18% of a resident's total property tax bill; the rest goes to the county, schools, fire districts and library, and those rates were not yet set. The mayor said the increase averages about 1.2 cents a year, or less than $3.50 a month for the average home, over the last six years including this one. Both described the year as unusually hard: health insurance premiums rose 29%, forcing the township to find an additional $2.4 million just for health coverage; liability insurance rose by $800,000; the winter's back-to-back storms cost more than $520,000 in cleanup; rock salt use was up 51% over a couple of years, gasoline 11% and diesel 49%; vehicle and equipment repairs rose about 34%; energy costs at township buildings rose about 17%; and COVID-era federal and state aid has ended. To limit the increase, he said, the township found more than $1.5 million in reductions, mostly by leaving some vacancies unfilled and pausing all new full-time hiring, and is leaning on state, county and federal grants for projects like James Monroe Park, the community garden and road paving.

The township's outside municipal auditor told the council he had reviewed the budget and called it a good result given the pressures, noting that his firm sees the same increases at the 135 towns it works with. He said the budget leaves about $2.4 million of room under the state's spending and levy caps unused, and does not draw on its "cap bank" of saved-up room. The budget goes to the state's Division of Local Government Services for review and, if all goes as expected, is adopted after a public hearing in May. Mr. McGowan said that based on last year's data Monroe had the second lowest overall tax rate in the county and expects to still be second lowest, and that even after planned water and sewer fee increases, the average household using under 25,000 gallons a quarter would still pay the lowest rates in the county and region. The mayor also highlighted the services the budget supports: more than 11,000 EMS calls in 2025, 72 sworn police officers, license plate reading cameras, and a senior center that recently reached 10,000 members.

2. A $2.4 million road bond is adopted, and officials explain what the township owes

The council adopted Ordinance 03-2026-006 by a 5–0 vote after a public hearing. It appropriates $2,556,000 for various improvements and authorizes about $2,433,800 in bonds or notes to pay for them. Bonds are long-term loans, and notes are short-term ones that the township can renew for up to several years before converting them to bonds. The biggest piece is the 2026 road paving program, followed by a street sweeper, $245,000 each in design costs for the 2026 roads and a paving project at Heritage Chase (which has a state Department of Transportation grant covering the construction), and equipment for a snow truck the township had already bought: hydraulics, a brine machine and a salt spreader. The business administrator estimated that permanent financing over about 10 years would cost around $285,000 a year, but the township's auditor clarified that the township expects to sell one-year notes in the next two months at a current market rate of about 2.3%, and would only convert to long-term bonds later, at today's long-term rates of about 3% to 3.5%.

A resident used the public comment period to ask how much the township owes, and officials answered in detail. Monthly debt service under the 2026 budget is $677,000, principal and interest combined. The township's outstanding bonds totaled $62.92 million in principal at the end of 2025. Debt service is below 10% of the budget, a level officials said the state's Department of Community Affairs considers healthy (the state starts asking questions closer to 25%). And the township's total debt is about 0.52% of the value of the property in town (roughly $15 billion, officials said), against a legal borrowing limit of 3.5%, which would be in the hundreds of millions of dollars. The same resident asked about "impact fees," charges on developers that would help towns pay for growth. Officials answered that the township already charges developers contributions for water and sewer improvements and for other items where state law allows, that the mayor writes to the legislature each year asking for broader authority (two more letters, one on impact fees and one on school funding, were released that day), and that a New Jersey Supreme Court ruling decades ago is why a legislative fix alone may not be enough.

3. The regional watershed study gets more expensive as towns drop out

The policy director of the Watershed Institute, a nonprofit that is organizing a regional study, asked the council to raise the township's share of the study. Every New Jersey town has to produce a plan, with draft reports due at the end of this year, for reducing water pollution and flooding, and Monroe had agreed in March to join a group of neighboring towns for a combined study. He said that more than 30% of the watershed has since declined to participate, which forced the costs to be reallocated: Monroe's share would rise from the roughly $48,000 the council had approved to roughly $53,000. Councilman Dipierro said the increase had first been about $10,000 before the engineers "sharpened their pencils" and brought it to about $5,000, and that even so it amounted to roughly 21% more for a project that had not yet started. The mayor asked what happens if more towns leave, and whether Monroe could end up paying for a study that its neighbors' problems undercut. The institute's representative said the study will identify each town's contribution to pollution, including towns that aren't participating, that the state's Department of Environmental Protection has indicated it will favor regional plans when awarding grants to build the projects, and that if another large town dropped out the approach might not work.

The council did not vote on the request that night; Council President Siegel suggested that the institute and the business administrator discuss it and bring back a recommendation. One resident urged the council to approve it without hesitation, saying the cost of neglecting local water is far greater. It came back as a resolution at the May 4 meeting, where the council approved an additional $4,036.

Also from Monday's meeting

Three residents ask the council to waive a gun permit fee

Three residents asked the council to waive, and in one case refund, the township's municipal portion of the fee for a permit to carry a firearm. One said that Englishtown had stopped charging its $150 municipal fee and refunded people back to January 2025 and that about 19 New Jersey towns have done something similar, argued that the Constitution protects the right to bear arms and that a right should not be taxed, and asked the council to consider it on a future agenda. Another, who described being retired on a fixed income, said renewals cost about $200 every two years. The third, a licensed permit holder who had raised the same issue in August, said he would pay about $400 for training and renewal in June and asked for a yes-or-no vote of the council. The council did not respond on the record. The question returned on May 4 (see our May 4 recap).

A proposed light pollution ordinance

A retired environmental scientist presented a proposed ordinance to limit light pollution (glare, light spilling onto neighboring property and sky glow), modeled on one adopted by Hopewell Township, and said 19 New Jersey towns have such rules. It would require shielded fixtures that point downward, limit light crossing property lines, use warmer color temperatures, and use timers or motion sensors. The speaker said residents had gathered about 200 signatures and handed a copy to the township clerk. There was no response on the record.

Contracts for soft costs and basin maintenance

A resident asked about the two $245,000 design contracts in the consent agenda and was told that they are for the township engineer's design work and that the construction itself will go out to bid separately. The resident also asked about a contract of about $188,700 for maintenance of stormwater basins. Officials said it is paid first from developers' escrow accounts for the basins and the rest from the general budget, because state rules require the township to maintain any basin that connects to its public storm sewer, and while the township has passed an ordinance shifting maintenance to individual communities where it can, it has no mechanism to go back to existing developments for more money.

Engineer's report

The township engineer reported that the James Monroe Park concession stand and park improvements should wrap up by the end of the month, and that the senior center pickleball courts and expanded parking lot should be done in four to six weeks. Construction on Lynx Drive, a $552,000 state grant project to mill and pave the road and add sidewalks and curbing on both sides, was to begin around the end of April and take about 90 days. The county was milling and paving parts of Perrineville, Butcher, Applegarth, Union Valley and Englishtown Roads. Plans for the 2026 township road program would be ready later that month, with bids and a recommended award at the May meeting. At the Prospect Plains Road and Applegarth Road intersection, a county project, work has been slowed because the utility poles must be moved, with the original pole layout sitting over a township sewer main, and the county is still waiting for revised plans from the power and phone companies.

Housekeeping

The consent agenda (resolutions 76 through 103, apart from one) passed. Councilman Dipierro asked that resolution 92 be voted on separately, and it passed with Dipierro abstaining. The council also approved the payment of claims for the period ending March 31, with Dipierro abstaining, and the minutes of the March 2 meeting. It proclaimed Parkinson's Awareness Month and a resident's 100th birthday, National Public Safety Telecommunications Week, Library Week and Arbor Day.

Remembering the township's construction official

Councilman Dipierro, Vice President Markel, who served with him on the township's ADA committee, and Mayor Dalina each paid tribute to the construction department's longtime director, who began as a plumbing inspector and rose to run the department. Dipierro said that everyone who dealt with the office, from residents to contractors, got help and respect, and that it took an hour to move through the line at the viewing. The mayor said he has always thought of township employees as family and described the man as the model of a professional who left "peace" in the hearts of those who knew him.

Council and mayor's remarks

Councilman Dipierro asked the township to ask American Water to check that its crews had not paved over valves or manholes after resurfacing a road, asked the engineer to press the railroad about the deteriorating asphalt at the Schoolhouse Road crossing, and asked about patched pavement on the bridge on the Old Bridge border. He also previewed the Holocaust remembrance program on April 19 at the senior center and Saturday's baseball and softball opening day. Councilman Van Dzura praised the budget work and the public works and utility crews, who handled potholes, water main breaks and fallen branches after a hard winter, reminded residents they can report potholes on the township website, and said an EMS crew reached a medical emergency he witnessed in about five minutes. Councilwoman Cohen said rising costs are also hurting local businesses and families, urged residents to donate to the local food bank, the food pantry at a Jamesburg church and the senior center, and noted the local Stop & Shop's remodel. Vice President Markel urged seniors to file their annual property tax reimbursement (PAS-1) application early, said about 450 people usually attend the Holocaust remembrance, and mentioned the National Day of Prayer on May 7. Council President Siegel said the township had sent letters to several bus companies about the Jersey City commuter service residents asked for at the March meeting, announced that Monroe had met with the Middlesex County Economic Development department about resources for local businesses and boards, congratulated the Concordia community association on being named a national "community of the year," and promoted a police safety presentation at the senior center on April 7 at 6:30 p.m.

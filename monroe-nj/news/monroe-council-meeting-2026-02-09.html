Town Council · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's February 9 council meeting

The power company answers for three outages and a street light backlog, the council holds its required public hearing on a new $1.9 million park off North Disbrow Hill Road, four affordable housing ordinances pass first reading, and the town takes stock of a storm that left three weeks of ice.

Feb 9, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel · 10 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, February 9, 2026; the recording runs about 2 hours 7 minutes, the agenda session adjourned at 6:57 p.m., and the meeting adjourned at 8:40 p.m.
Who was there
Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dipierro, Council Vice President Michael Markel, Council President Rupa Siegel, Mayor Stephen Dalina and Business Administrator Kevin McGowan. Councilman Terence Van Dzura was absent, so four of the five council members voted.

Monroe's council meets in two parts on the same night: an agenda session, where the council reads through the items and residents can ask about them, and a regular meeting, where the votes happen. An ordinance is a permanent local law that must be voted on twice: first reading is the introduction, and second reading, after a public hearing, is the vote to adopt. A resolution is a one-time official decision, and routine ones are bundled into a single vote called the consent agenda. This night's biggest items were not votes at all: a utility presentation, a state-required public hearing on a park, and a long look back at the January blizzard.

1. Jersey Central Power & Light answers for the outages and the street lights

Two representatives of Jersey Central Power & Light (JCP&L), the township's electric utility, came at the business administrator's invitation to answer concerns officials say they had raised for months. They walked through three recent outages. A wind event on November 16 hit about 5,000 customers and was restored in about 10 minutes; a second wind event on November 23 hit about 2,000 customers for about 80 minutes; and on December 3 a large tree outside the utility's trimming zone fell across a high-voltage line, knocking out about 2,000 customers for about 20 minutes. They explained that when a protective device on a line trips, crews must walk the whole line before power can be restored, even if they find nothing, because the device may have stopped a fault that would have burned the line down; balloons with metal coatings, they said, are a common culprit.

The company said it is putting about $4.7 million into Monroe's system by the end of 2028 under a program it calls Energize New Jersey: four "circuit ties" that let one circuit back-feed another during an outage, about seven automated protection devices, three substation upgrades, and pole, equipment and wire replacement on three circuits, two of them the ones hit by the November outages. It also promised a 60-day notice and a street list before tree trimming begins on the Monroe substation's four-year cycle, and said it would provide talking points for the township website. One of the two representatives said the company assigned a dedicated representative to the township's homeowners' associations last fall, because those associations had been calling the township instead of the utility, and that JCP&L has been restructured so that local decisions, rather than its Ohio-based parent, drive where it invests.

Street lights drew the most pointed questions. A council member said they had entered the same dark street lights on the utility's website three or four times; another asked whether the homeowners' association representative could report back to associations on which lights were being fixed, since residents in adult communities were angry. The utility said it repaired 90 street lights the evening after the last meeting with the administration, that reports submitted by home address sometimes point to the wrong pole, and that the slowest repairs are buried-cable faults, which require marking out other utilities' lines and a private contractor, a job made harder by weeks of ice. It also said a light that nobody reports will not get fixed, and that converting to LED fixtures would require a formal request from the township and a fee under the state-approved tariff, which the speaker estimated at "a few hundred dollars" per fixture. Later, in answer to residents, the company said residents can report a light on its website by cross streets and a 500-character note when the pole number is unavailable, by phone, or through its Facebook page. It said generation makes up roughly 60% of a bill and is passed through at cost, that the company owns no generators and does not profit from the recent increase, and that it believes data centers should bring their own power rather than push up residential bills.

2. The council opens its Green Acres hearing on a new park off North Disbrow Hill Road

The state's Green Acres program requires a public hearing before a town applies for open-space grants, and Monroe held one for its plan to build the first phase of a new park at 80 North Disbrow Hill Road, about 21 acres near Butcher Road. The township's engineer described a fully inclusive playground, a parking lot, walking paths, a stormwater basin and sidewalks, with possible sports fields later. The estimated cost is $1,876,000, and the application asks Green Acres for half of it, $938,000. The township is also applying for a smaller state recreation grant for the rest. The engineer said no state wetland permits are needed for this first phase, that only about 8 of the 21 acres are dry land that can be built on, that the wetlands and their buffers will not be touched, and that, given how long past Green Acres applications have taken, construction is more likely in 2027.

The site is part of the settlement that stopped a warehouse on the other side of North Disbrow Hill Road. Officials told residents that the township is buying this parcel for $2,450,000 (approved by the council last year) with a contract to be signed within days and closing later this year, that it is also receiving 44 acres of open space for free, and that the affordable housing development coming to the other side carries a 30% set-aside. Residents who spoke asked about wetlands, a nearby stream that feeds the Millstone River, whether the township owns the land yet, bathrooms (none in phase one), sidewalks on the narrow roads (conceptually planned, though environmentally sensitive areas near Butcher Road may limit them) and developer contributions. A nearby homeowner thanked the council for stopping the warehouse. Among the consent items was $200,000 for design and bidding costs for a separate community garden that has a $1.2 million Green Acres grant behind it; officials put the garden's total cost at about $2.3 million and said it should go out to bid this spring.

3. Four affordable housing ordinances pass first reading, and residents ask what they add up to

The council introduced four ordinances tied to the township's fourth-round affordable housing plan. A first reading only starts the process, so none of them were adopted that night. Ordinance 02-2026-003 rezones the sites in the plan, 004 amends the highway development and residential-agricultural zones, and 005 rewrites the township's affordable housing fee rules to match the state's amended Fair Housing Act; a fourth, 02-2026-002, approves traffic signal improvements at Applegarth Road and Joan Warren Way. All four passed without a no vote that we could hear. They were adopted after public hearings on March 2 (see our March 2 recap).

In the agenda session, officials described the four sites: one on Buckelew Avenue across from the Regency community, planned as a senior community; the former Meadow View Care Center at 319 Spotswood-Gravel Hill Road, planned as 100% affordable; a site on the southeast side of town bordering Manalapan, planned as a mixed development with some affordable homes; and the former "bald eagle" site next to East Windsor, which already had affordable-housing zoning and will now be a much smaller project than the one proposed years ago. Later, a resident asked for the totals. Officials said the four sites add up to about 383 homes, with 22 affordable homes in a 100-home site, 20 in a 67-home site, all 30 in the township-sponsored Meadow View building, and 37 in a 186-home site, about 109 affordable homes in all. The fourth round runs until June 2035. The same resident urged the council to make sure the affordable homes, rather than only the market-rate homes, get built.

Also from Monday's meeting

The storm and how the township handled it

The business administrator gave a detailed account of the winter storm that began around January 25: public works, the municipal utilities authority and parks crews plowed and treated more than 150 miles of roads around the clock from midnight Sunday through Tuesday, working 12-hour and then 15-hour shifts with 25 to 30 pieces of equipment per shift and about 525 tons of salt, plus a subcontractor. He said the township was closed for the emergency on Monday, January 26, that the snow then froze into ice that lasted about three weeks, and that crews kept clearing bus stops, crossing-guard locations and sidewalks afterward. He also explained enforcement: the township ordinance requires owners to clear sidewalks and fire hydrants on their property, but zoning officers respond mainly to complaints (for example, a sidewalk on a school-bus route) and have been issuing reminders, not fines or court summonses, and residents who can't do the work were asked to call the township. Councilman Dipierro asked whether the township is reimbursed for storm costs; the answer was no, since a governor's emergency declaration does not bring money unless the federal government steps in. Council members thanked the crews and residents, and a resident later suggested a pilot program to give snow crews a paid day off, which the council did not address.

Questions about payments and a drone show

Before voting to approve the bill list, a council member asked about a purchase of 5,000 pencils for $7,771 and a $3,000 deposit to a production company. The business administrator explained the township's four-step purchasing process, and officials said the pencils were requested by the cultural arts commission to give away at events and the deposit holds a drone show planned for the township's 250th anniversary celebrations alongside fireworks. The bill list was approved over Councilman Dipierro's no vote. The council also approved the minutes of its December 22 and January 5 meetings.

Supervisors' raises, consent items and parking

The council adopted, 4–0, the 2026 salary ordinance for supervisory personnel, which provides raises of 2.9% across the board, the same as in recent years. The consent agenda (resolutions 31 through 55, except two pulled for separate votes) passed. Residents asked about several items: a parking lot expansion behind the community center rink that adds about 70 spaces and a new loop road that exits onto Mammoth Road; the appointment of four police officers as humane enforcement officers to investigate animal cruelty; and the township's representatives on the regional flood control commission, who receive no extra pay. One resident suggested the township review development fees as the municipal utilities authority's costs rise.

Engineer and the watershed study

The township engineer said the cold slowed work on the James Monroe Park concession stand and on the senior center pickleball courts, parking lot and pavilion. He also described Monroe's state stormwater permit, which requires an inventory (finished at the end of 2025), an assessment report due at the end of 2026 and a watershed improvement plan due at the end of 2027. A Watershed Institute proposal would have the roughly 10 towns that share the upper Millstone River hire one consultant, and he said Monroe would need to pass a resolution to join and that cost was still a question; Councilman Dipierro said Monroe's share could be about 20%. A Watershed Institute representative reminded the council that a deadline to commit was approaching. The council joined the study on March 2 and the price rose by April 6 (see our March 2 and April 6 recaps).

Public comments and remarks

In the open comment period, residents also asked the township to remind owners to clear fire hydrants and mailbox access, to remind residents when property taxes are due and to warn about water-line insurance marketing; asked whether solar street lights are possible (the utility said the panels residents have seen on poles belong to a separate state program and do not power the lights); reported an uneven manhole where Jessica Drive meets Butcher Road and icy conditions on Fern Drive, which officials said is a county road. Councilman Dipierro asked for "no skating" signs at stormwater basins, praised a tour of the floating solar array on the municipal utilities authority's lake, reminded residents of the February 21 fire district elections (2 to 9 p.m., or by mail), and asked to be told of upcoming court dates in township lawsuits; the attorney replied that the BFI landfill case is settled and only the redevelopment obligations remain. Councilwoman Cohen announced that the advisory health council is distributing "File of Life" forms residents can post on a refrigerator to list their medications for emergency responders. The council president said a Monroe resident was competing as a snowboarder at the Winter Olympics, and the clerk explained that, since March 1, the township's legal notices are published online on the township website, not in newspapers.

Honors and announcements

The mayor and the police chief honored a 25-year building maintenance worker at the police station who retired on January 31, and the council proclaimed American Heart Month and the February 25 birthday of a resident turning 103. The mayor announced a fall-prevention and balance program on March 24 at the senior center and a rabies clinic on March 7 at the community center, and the council's next meeting was set for March 2.

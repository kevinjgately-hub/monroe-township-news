Town Council · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's March 2 council meeting

A divided council adopts the rezoning that implements Monroe's fourth-round affordable housing plan, including 30 wheelchair-accessible apartments at the former Meadow View Care Center that neighbors fought, as commuters ask for a bus to Jersey City and the town digs out from a blizzard.

Mar 2, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, March 2, 2026; the recording runs about 1 hour 59 minutes, the agenda session adjourned at 7:11 p.m., and the meeting adjourned at 8:28 p.m.
Who was there
Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dipierro, Councilman Terence Van Dzura, Council Vice President Michael Markel, Council President Rupa Siegel, Mayor Stephen Dalina and Business Administrator Kevin McGowan. All five council members were present.

Monroe's council meets in two parts on the same night: an agenda session, where the council reads through the items and residents can ask about them, and a regular meeting, where the votes happen. An ordinance is a permanent local law that must be voted on twice: first reading is the introduction, and second reading, after a public hearing, is the vote to adopt. A resolution is a one-time official decision, and routine ones are bundled into a single vote called the consent agenda. Four ordinances were up for adoption this night, all tied to the state's affordable housing rules, and the first one drew a crowd.

1. The council adopts the zoning for its fourth-round affordable housing plan, over neighbors' objections

New Jersey requires every town to provide its share of affordable housing, and the obligations are set in ten-year "rounds." Monroe is in Round 4. Officials explained that the township's obligation is 76 homes to be rehabilitated and 460 "credits" of new affordable housing, and that because some kinds of housing count extra (group homes and fully affordable projects count double, for example), the township expects to meet it by actually building or counting 345 homes. Ordinance 02-2026-003 rezones a set of properties to carry out that plan: in total it provides for 383 homes, 109 of them affordable (about 29%), and roughly 167 of them for sale rather than rent. The council adopted it, as amended, on a 4–1 vote; as best the captions can tell, Councilman Dipierro cast the no vote. The amendment, which Councilman Van Dzura proposed, removed language in the age-restricted (55-and-over) housing section that would have allowed adult children aged 19 or older to live there, which he said no other age-restricted community in town allows. Resolution 60, endorsing the housing plan the planning board had amended the week before after the Fair Share Housing Center challenged the township's first version and asked for more newly built homes, was part of the consent agenda. Business Administrator Kevin McGowan thanked the council after the votes for finishing roughly two years of work, and said the mayor's negotiations had cut the number of market-rate homes the plan requires.

The most contested part was a single site: 319 Spotswood-Gravel Hill Road, the former Meadow View Care Center, which the township bought in 2023 with its affordable housing trust fund. About a half dozen neighbors spoke against the zoning as written, and a few more voiced the same objections in the regular meeting. They said they did not oppose affordable housing in principle but that 30 apartments next to single-family homes would bring many more cars and children, parking on their street, a loss of privacy and lighting and noise from a three-story building behind their yards, and that there are no sidewalks, playground or parking nearby. Several described problems when the site was a care facility, including trespassing, frequent police and ambulance calls and a death in the building. They asked for enforceable setbacks, step-downs in height, permanent landscaped buffers, limits on rear-facing windows and balconies, impact studies before the rezoning rather than after, and in a few cases property tax relief for the closest homes; one neighbor said they had not received a legal notice despite living about 325 feet away, and others suggested building near Route 33 or a shopping center, or building single-family homes instead.

The mayor and business administrator responded that the township worked with the state to get the care center closed, bought the property specifically to keep that kind of facility from coming back, and plans to partner with a nonprofit developer, Project Freedom, on independent-living apartments for people of all ages that are designed for wheelchair users, similar to the township's veterans housing on Cranberry-Halfacre Road. They said officials had visited the developer's buildings in West Windsor and Hamilton. The rezoning allows a building up to three stories and 45 feet, with 30-foot side and rear yards and a 50-foot front yard, and officials stressed that it only sets the zoning: any actual project must go through a full site plan review with public hearings before the planning board, which they said will not happen this year and could be years away. If the nonprofit pulled out, the township would have to find another affordable housing nonprofit, and it cannot make the site senior-only because it has already reached the cap on senior units it can count. The mayor compared the reaction to the uproar when a different group home, Parker House, arrived in a neighborhood years ago, and said it became a good neighbor.

2. Two more housing ordinances pass, and a long argument over what the numbers mean

The council also adopted Ordinance 02-2026-004 and Ordinance 02-2026-005, both required to keep the township's rules in line with the state's amended Fair Housing Act. The first adds statutory language to the highway development and R3A residential-agricultural zones at the request of the Fair Share Housing Center, including a requirement that 13% of affordable homes be set aside for very-low-income households, and replaces references to the old state Council on Affordable Housing, which no longer exists, with the state's current "Uniform Housing Affordability Controls." The second repeals and replaces the township's affordable housing and development fee sections. One resident asked whether the first would turn open space into housing, and officials said no: it changes no zoning, and the township's deed-restricted open space can't be built on in any zone. Neither ordinance drew other opposition, and both passed.

Several residents objected to the way the numbers were presented. One said the resolution endorsing the plan says 460 "units" while the plan says 345, called the explanation condescending, and asked the council to table the ordinance until the wording was clear. Officials said the 460 figure is credits, the term the state uses, that the housing plan itself is the binding document that lays out both numbers, and that the ordinance wording matches state law. Another resident asked how many affordable homes built under the previous round are actually occupied and whether market-rate homes are being completed before the affordable ones. Officials said the state's rules bar the township from issuing occupancy certificates for market-rate homes until the required affordable homes are built, that the township once held up seven market-rate certificates on that basis, and that they believe the 53 affordable homes at Georgetown Estates and 66 senior homes on Spotswood-Englishtown Road are full or nearly full, though they don't manage them.

3. Commuters ask the township to help bring back a bus to Jersey City

Four commuters asked the council for help restoring bus service from Monroe to Jersey City, which they said stopped after COVID. They said they now drive to a transit stop near the Turnpike or all the way into Jersey City, losing 25 minutes each way at the transfer and spending $50 to $60 a day, or about $1,000 a month, on parking. They represent colleagues at several large financial employers and said that a petition drive among a limited group of people had gathered 100 signatures and that a survey found interest from 100 or more riders. They asked the township to press NJ Transit and the private coach company to reinstate the route, at least as a pilot, and one said riders would pay for the bus if 15 people rode each way.

Mr. McGowan said the township has asked NJ Transit and the coach company in the past, most recently some time in 2025, and been told that they have decided against it for their own profit-based reasons. He said the mayor and council don't control those decisions, but that the township will take the ridership data and signatures and advocate on residents' behalf. On April 6, Council President Siegel reported that the township had sent letters to several bus companies (see our April 6 recap).

Also from Monday's meeting

Consent agenda and a cancelled bond balance

The consent agenda (resolutions 56 through 75, except two pulled for separate votes) passed 4–1 on the votes we could hear, with Councilman Dipierro voting no. Residents asked about several items, and officials explained them. Resolution 64 cancels about $645,000 left over from a $1.2 million water and sewer bond from 2014 that was meant for a water main to the Route 613 tank that turned out not to be needed, which frees the money for other water and sewer capital work. Resolution 62 applies for a state grant of up to $300,000 for the former care center site, including about $50,000 to put in a power-operated accessible restroom door, which officials said is a conservative estimate and any leftover would be reallocated. Other items renewed the water treatment utility's salt supply, bought water meters (about $10 more per meter than last year) and a sewer odor-and-corrosion control chemical. Resolution 74 commits the township to join the Watershed Institute's regional study of the upper Millstone watershed, which came back with a higher price on April 6.

Temporary budget appropriations

A resident asked why the council was approving "temporary" appropriations, including $45,000 for landfill costs. Officials explained that in January a town can spend 35% of the prior year's budget under a temporary budget, and that after that, extra emergency temporary appropriations are added each March so bills can be paid until the budget is adopted in April and May, which is standard practice. The landfill money is for tipping fees at the county landfill in East Brunswick, not the former BFI landfill, whose settlement is done.

Work on private developments

A resident returned to a comment a township attorney made late last year that a developer who starts underground site work before inspections is doing so "at its own risk," arguing the risk is largely the town's, since buried utilities that settle or break later can become the township's problem, and asked the township to find ways to reduce it. The council did not respond on the record. (The same resident raised it again on June 3.)

Streetlights, school board and traffic

A resident thanked the council for having a power company representative at an earlier meeting and asked the township to press the company to analyze replacing, not just converting, streetlight fixtures when it switches to LEDs. The same resident said the Board of Education's investigation involving the superintendent, which the resident said has been underway since August, is not moving quickly enough, asked the council to urge the board to hurry, asked the council to lobby the state for more school aid, and asked the township to fix a bump where Jessica Drive meets Butcher Road that makes cars drift onto the shoulder. The council president said the road belongs to the county and the township would follow up with it.

Engineer's report

The township engineer said the snow slowed work on the James Monroe Park concession stand and the senior center pickleball courts and parking lot, though crews are working inside and have put up the pavilion. The scaffolding is up on the Route 522 water tower for painting, and a water and sewer main project on a road between Jamesburg and Monroe, which has trenches and temporary paving, is expected to be done by the end of the month, with Councilman Dipierro asking for curb-to-curb repaving afterward. Officials said the 2026 budget will be presented in a slide show at the April meeting, with the adoption hearing in May.

Housekeeping

The council approved the payment of claims (run date February 25, with Councilman Dipierro abstaining) and the minutes of the February 9 meeting. It proclaimed the Indian Cultural Association of Monroe's 20th anniversary, a resident's 102nd birthday and Save the Soil Day (March 21). The first reading of a $2.56 million bond ordinance for paving, a street sweeper and related items passed and was adopted at the April 6 meeting (see our April 6 recap).

Snow, schools and remarks

Council members and the mayor thanked police, fire, public works, the utility, parks crews and the office of emergency management after what the mayor called the biggest snowstorm in decades, with a second snowfall the following Wednesday that he said was worse. Councilman Dipierro said it was difficult to clear every driveway apron but that main roads were the priority, and encouraged parents to sign children up for baseball, softball and hockey at the community center. Councilwoman Cohen and Vice President Markel were headed to local elementary schools for Read Across America, and Markel said he had presented a proclamation to a resident who turned 103. The mayor read a thank-you letter to residents for the way neighbors helped each other, and noted a rabies clinic on March 7, MakerFest on March 21 and an egg hunt on March 28; he also paid tribute to a World War II veteran who died the week before and would have turned 100 on March 22. The township clerk reminded residents that, as of March 1, legal advertisements are posted under "legal notices" on the township website's homepage.

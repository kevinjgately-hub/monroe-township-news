Board of Education · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's November 18 Board of Education meeting

The district gets its first scores from the state's new adaptive test, which officials say can't be compared with past years. The board invokes the doctrine of necessity three more times, rejects four legal checks again, and hears sharp criticism of short committee meetings.

Nov 18, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the school district's YouTube channel · 9 min read

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Board of Education, regular monthly public meeting (after a closed session)
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Wednesday, November 18, 2026; the public recording runs about 2 hours 8 minutes
Who was there
Vice President Carmen Alvarez ran the meeting because Board President Gail DiPane was ill. Members Vanessa Forst, Eric Marozine, Kate Rattner, Usha Sharma, Peter Tufano and Charles Zeichner and Jamesburg representative Dr. Amanda Peck were present. Member Peter Piro did not attend. Acting Superintendent Dr. Adam Layman, Business Administrator and Board Secretary Laura Allen and board attorney Adam Weiss also took part.

This was the board's first regular meeting since its November 9 special meeting on Superintendent Dr. Chari Chanley, who remains on paid leave, and since this month's school board election. The board met in closed session first, on litigation and a harassment, intimidation and bullying (HIB) investigation involving students. Nothing about the superintendent was announced in public. Here is everything that happened, biggest stories first.

1. First results from the state's new adaptive test, and a warning not to compare

Dr. Layman gave the district's annual test-score presentation, with a large caveat. Last spring was the first year of the state's new NJSLA tests, the first brand-new state test since 2015. He said the tests are built on the 2023 learning standards rather than the 2014 ones, written by a new company with all-new questions, and "adaptive": a student who answers correctly gets harder questions, and one who misses gets easier ones. Score scales and cut-off points changed too, so the state has asked districts not to compare this year's results with earlier years, even though its own reporting templates still put them side by side. Dr. Layman said the fair comparison this year is Monroe against the state on the same test, and that Monroe continues to outperform the state. He said that applies whether a score went up or down: neither change can be read as student growth or decline. One example: 89.3% of eighth graders who took the geometry test scored at the top level (Level 5). He noted that because more than half of Monroe's eighth graders take algebra or geometry tests instead, the regular eighth-grade math results don't represent the whole grade.

Other numbers from the presentation:

  • Advanced Placement: about 100 more AP exams were taken than the year before. 91% earned a qualifying score (3 or higher), up about 3 points, and 69% scored a 4 or 5, up about 7 points. Two new AP courses, Business with Personal Finance and Cybersecurity, are being offered.
  • Bullying: 24 HIB investigations last school year, 14 founded and 10 unfounded, versus 25 the year before.
  • English learners: of 156 students assessed, speaking more than 30 languages, 38 reached the exit score and 37 left the program.
  • Alternate assessment: 46 students with significant disabilities took the alternate test in English and math, and 23 in science.

Asked by a resident whether the results raised concerns, Dr. Layman said the state results arrive months late, so the district relies on its own MAP tests, given two to three times a year, to place students in interventions from the first weeks of school. The full presentation is to be posted on the district website.

2. Three more doctrine-of-necessity votes, and the legal checks

Mr. Weiss read three resolutions invoking the doctrine of necessity, which lets conflicted members vote when too many have conflicts for the board to act otherwise (see our October 14 recap). They covered paying the insurer-assigned defense firm Flanagan, Barone & O'Brien, paying Schenck, Price, Smith & King (the firm handling matters related to Dr. Chanley's employment), and "financial and employment related matters" concerning Dr. Chanley. After a question from Ms. Rattner, Mr. Weiss added her to the first resolution's list of conflicted members. All three passed with the same split. Ms. Alvarez, Ms. Forst, Dr. Peck, Ms. Sharma and Mr. Zeichner voted yes, Mr. Marozine and Mr. Tufano voted no, and Ms. Rattner abstained.

Before one vote, Mr. Tufano asked whether the board's special counsel had advised conflicted members to keep voting no on legal bills he said the board is contractually obligated to pay, noting that the board attorney had advised otherwise. Mr. Weiss cautioned members about disclosing confidential attorney-client advice, and no one answered. On the bill list, checks 17670 through 17673 failed again, as they did in October, with Ms. Alvarez and Ms. Sharma voting no, Ms. Forst and Ms. Rattner abstaining and Mr. Zeichner's vote garbled in the captions. Check 19531, which failed in October and which Ms. Rattner's recusal identified as relating to Dr. Chanley, passed this time. Ms. Allen said this month's bills for the insurance-appointed defense attorneys total $57,758.16, and that the board attorney's monthly bill had not arrived in time for the meeting.

3. A $192,849 credit on the Applegarth roof, and a Mill Lake roof next

The board approved a credit of $192,848.76 on the Applegarth School roof replacement. Ms. Allen said the original contract was $2,342,548, including an allowance for unforeseen conditions that never came up, so the final contract is $2,149,699.24. Because the project was partly funded by a state grant (a ROD grant), she said the unspent portion goes back to the state rather than to other projects. The board also authorized sending plans for a partial roof replacement at Mill Lake School to the state Department of Education for approval. Dr. Layman said the project is in the district's long-range facilities plan and that how much to put in next year's budget will be decided in the next phase. A resident put its cost at about $2 million; officials did not give a figure.

Reading Mr. Piro's facilities report, Ms. Alvarez said school solar systems at several sites, including Oak Tree and the administration and transportation buildings, were connected to the grid during the teachers' convention break. JCP&L postponed the connections for the high school and Woodland School because of bad weather on November 6, and the solar projects remain on schedule to go live by the end of 2026.

Also from Wednesday's meeting

Health insurance and the budget

Answering a resident's questions, Ms. Allen said that the district has spent about what it expected on health insurance so far and hopes for lower rates for the second half of the school year, with more detail promised for December. She said the new staff contracts' raises came in higher than budgeted, but savings from dropping the most expensive health-plan tier will cover the difference. She also said a drop in general-fund cash from about $20 million to $17 million over a month is normal ebb and flow.

A climate science grant and policy changes

Mr. Zeichner reported that the district won a $45,863 science grant for after-school and summer programs on climate change for grades 6–12, starting in summer 2027, with Rutgers as a partner and money for a greenhouse. The district's integrated preschool program is full. Ms. Rattner reported that several policies are up for a first reading: rules for filling board vacancies after recent court decisions; changes tied to a new state law on internet-enabled devices; home instruction for a health condition, which now kicks in after 17 cumulative days of absence instead of 20; mental-health resources printed on student IDs for grades 7–12; and requirements for volunteer coaches and club advisors. Applications for the board's citizens advisory committee, open to residents and high school students, are due November 30. The Monroe Education Foundation's teacher grants, totaling $14,743, were accepted. Board members also thanked donors of two pianos and about $2,500 in school supplies.

Sharp words in public comment

A former board president, who said she had been elected to the board again this month, criticized what she called short, discussion-free committee meetings. She said the finance committee met for 2 minutes and 12 seconds in a year of high tax increases, and said some members don't know enough about district programs. In a second turn, she accused members of pursuing "a vendetta" by voting for the doctrine of necessity and then against bills she said they are legally required to pay. Mr. Zeichner, who chairs the curriculum committee, said he strongly disagreed and defended the staff on the committee; she replied that her criticism was aimed at board members, not staff. Another resident thanked the board for what she called "restoring ethical leadership," credited it with full-day kindergarten, and said legal costs are being driven by the former president and her allies. She said court challenges over the superintendent's suspension had failed and that the former president had been reprimanded by the School Ethics Commission and is appealing. These are the speakers' own claims; we have not verified them and take no side. Another resident asked about the Title I school plans, which Dr. Layman said are annual parent-involvement documents for the district's Title I schools, not board policies. The minutes of the November 9 special meeting will be up for approval in December.

Student reports

Student board members reported on American Education Week visits and Veterans Day programs at the elementary schools. They also highlighted fundraisers for breast cancer research, the Wounded Warrior Project and local food pantries, the marching band's first grand-champion award, a GMC title and state sectional win for the boys soccer team, and the football team's best season in years. They said the cellphone rule continues to go well.

Coming up

The next board meeting is Wednesday, December 16, 2026. The board's annual reorganization meeting, when newly elected members take office, is Monday, January 4, 2027.

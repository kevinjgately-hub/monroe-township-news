Town Council

Council bans data centers — for now — as residents pack the room over electric bills

Monroe's council also voted to turn the old town landfill into a solar farm, borrowed nearly $7 million for roads and pipes, and swore in three new police officers, at a meeting that ran two and a half hours.

Aug 3, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel

What
Monroe Township Council, combined agenda & regular meeting
Where
Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Monday, August 3, 2026, 6:39–8:56 p.m.
Who was there
Councilwoman Miriam Cohen, Councilman Charles Dapiro, Council Vice President Michael Markell, Council President Rupa Seagull. Councilman Terrence Vanzura was absent.

If you've never sat through a town council meeting, here's the basic shape of one: elected council members vote on permanent local laws, called ordinances, and on smaller one-time official decisions, called resolutions. Before most votes, anyone in the room gets a few minutes to speak. That's exactly what happened in Monroe Township on August 3 — and this time, a lot of people showed up to talk about one thing in particular: data centers.

A new rule: no data centers in Monroe, at least for now

A data center is a large building full of computer servers — the machines that power things like artificial intelligence tools and cloud storage. Running and cooling all those servers takes enormous amounts of electricity and water, and as AI companies expand, they're increasingly asking towns like Monroe to host them.

The council passed an ordinance this week making clear that data centers are not an allowed use of land anywhere in town, closing a loophole in decades-old zoning language that didn't clearly address them one way or the other. Township officials said the point isn't to rule out data centers forever, but to buy time to write real rules before any company can propose one.

Public comment on this ran long. Several residents connected data centers directly to their own rising electric bills — one woman told the council her bill jumped roughly 20% over the past year, and that she'd had to go without heat some nights this past winter because she couldn't afford to run it. Others raised concerns about New Jersey's ongoing drought and the large volumes of water data centers use for cooling. A few residents pushed back a little on the alarm, arguing that AI-driven infrastructure is coming whether Monroe hosts it or not, and that trying to block it outright — rather than regulate it carefully — could mean missing out on jobs and tax revenue. Township officials said Monroe currently has no plans to offer data center operators any tax or utility discounts, which is something some other towns have controversially done.

The town's old landfill is becoming a solar farm

Monroe used to run its own landfill — essentially the town dump — on about 80 acres, until it closed sometime around the 1980s. Because of contamination, it was designated a federal Superfund site, meaning the federal Environmental Protection Agency had to sign off on who was responsible for managing it going forward. That responsibility fell to a waste company, later absorbed into a larger corporation, which the township eventually sued.

The council adopted a redevelopment plan this week that rezones the old landfill site to allow only one new use: a large community solar array. The land is being transferred to a solar company as part of settling that lawsuit. A community solar project lets residents "subscribe" to a share of the solar farm's output and get a credit on their own electric bill, without needing to install panels on their own roof — and township officials confirmed joining a community solar program wouldn't stop anyone from also putting solar on their own home later. The project still needs a full site plan approved by the town's planning board before any construction happens.

Not everyone was reassured. One resident who says he was a Monroe police officer for 33 years told the council he personally witnessed tanker trucks dumping chemicals at the landfill decades ago, and that he believes it's connected to cancer cases in the surrounding neighborhood. He asked the town to test the ground before installing any solar equipment.

Worth noting: that account is one person's decades-old recollection, not an independently confirmed finding, and no evidence was presented at the meeting to verify it either way. Township officials did confirm that a permanent pump system has been removing contaminated water (called "leachate," the liquid that seeps through a closed landfill) from the site for years and will keep doing so indefinitely, regardless of who owns or operates the property.

The town is borrowing nearly $7 million

Council also approved two bond ordinances — essentially the town taking out a loan, to be paid back over time, which is a standard way local governments pay for large projects instead of raising taxes all at once. About $2.4 million was approved for capital projects like road paving and police equipment, and about $4.4 million for water and sewer pipe work. Officials noted that each individual project funded this way still has to come back to the council separately for approval before any money is actually spent — this vote just secures the financing.

Also on the agenda

Before any of that, Police Chief Banos swore in three new officers — Ryan Risco, a former professional cage fighter and Middlesex County corrections officer; Riley Oille, a state wrestling finalist; and Cameron O'Neal — all recent graduates of their police academies, now assigned to patrol.

Council members also used the meeting to flag smaller neighborhood issues: dead tree limbs hanging over several roads, reminders that residents need a town permit before cutting down a tree, and repeated frustration with the regional utility company, JCP&L, over delays moving utility poles and fixing streetlights near ongoing road projects. The town's new pickleball courts at the senior center are essentially finished, and — despite some confusion caused by a front-desk miscommunication — are open to the public on weekends. And this fall marks the 25th anniversary of September 11; the town's annual remembrance ceremony will be held as usual at Memorial Tree Park.

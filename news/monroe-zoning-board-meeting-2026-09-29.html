Zoning Board · Full Meeting Recap

Everything that happened at Monroe's September 29 Zoning Board meeting

A gun shop at the Recoil shooting range wins approval to sell firearms and ammunition, the new Chipotle on Route 33 is approved after a long argument over parking and a bike lane on a private road, and the board formalizes one earlier decision.

Sep 29, 2026 · Compiled from the official meeting recording, published on the township's YouTube channel

Quick facts

What
Monroe Township Zoning Board of Adjustment, regular monthly meeting
Where
Monroe Township Municipal Building, Monroe Township, Middlesex County, New Jersey
When
Tuesday, September 29, 2026; the recording runs about 3 hours 13 minutes
Who was there
Seven members voted on the main applications. The township's website lists Vincent LaFata as chairman and Louis Masters as vice chairman. The captions garble most members' names, so we are not listing the rest.

The Zoning Board of Adjustment is the township body that hears requests from property owners for relief from the zoning rules. The two most common kinds are a "D1" or use variance (permission to use land in a way the zoning for that area doesn't normally allow) and a "bulk" or "C" variance (permission to bend a measurement rule, like a setback, parking-space size or sign size). Applicants and their experts testify under oath, the board's own professionals weigh in, the public can speak, and the board votes. The board did not meet in July or August; the township's website shows both of those meetings as canceled, which is why this was the board's first meeting since June 30. It approved the minutes of that June meeting and then took up two applications.

1. The Recoil shooting range wins approval to sell guns and ammunition

Recoil New Jersey, Inc. has run a 15-lane indoor shooting range on Mott Avenue (block 95, lot 1.01, a 12.6-acre property that includes wetlands) for about six years. It came back asking to add a roughly 10-to-11-foot building addition for retail sales of firearms and ammunition, to raise the number of parking spaces from 29 to 56, and to add a second, two-way driveway. Before the board could take up the merits, it had to decide a threshold question. The applicant had twice before been turned down for retail sales, and a board generally can't rehear a request it has already decided unless the new application is substantially different. Lawyers call that doctrine res judicata, and the board voted 7–0 that the changes were different enough to hear it. The board then approved the application 7–0. It needed a use variance, because the zone doesn't ordinarily allow retail sales, plus several smaller variances: a front buffer of 50.5 feet instead of 60, parking stalls of 9 by 18 feet instead of 10 by 20, and a loading area of 10 by 25 feet instead of 12 by 50.

The owner testified that the hours would not change: 10 a.m. to 9 p.m. on weekdays, 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. on Saturday and 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. on Sunday. In answers to board questions, the applicant's side said the store would not sell automatic weapons (which New Jersey prohibits), would not rent guns, would sell handguns, rifles and shotguns, would follow the state's 10-round magazine limit, and would not sell black powder. A Jamesburg police captain testified that his department trains at the range 10 to 16 days a year and has done so for about four years, and a traffic expert and a planner also testified for the applicant. The board engineer agreed to waive the usual fence around the stormwater basin, and he asked that some lighting stay on overnight rather than turning everything off from dusk to dawn, a detail to be worked out with the police. Conditions include a sidewalk from Mott Avenue to the building, two electric-vehicle chargers protected by bollards, an extended retaining wall, a clear "sight triangle" at the driveway, a full lighting plan, conservation easements before the township releases the developer's bond, and all earlier conditions staying in force. Members of the public spoke on both sides. A Monroe High School senior who competes in pistol shooting and his mother spoke in favor of the shop, and one resident objected to a gun store in a residential area. The board's attorney noted that the board has the legal authority to grant use variances.

2. A Chipotle replaces the bank planned for Route 33 and Applegarth Road

The second application came from a company the captions identify as the Verde Group. It asked to replace a previously approved 3,500-square-foot bank, which would have had three drive-thru lanes, with a 2,385-square-foot Chipotle with a single pickup-only drive-thru lane, in the village-center zone near Route 33 and Applegarth Road. The drive-thru is the part that needed a D1 use variance. The restaurant also needed several bulk variances, including a shallow lot (about 100 feet deep where the zoning calls for 200, a condition of the existing lot), a building set back 45 feet from the front where the zone's maximum is 20, and a freestanding sign of about 205 square feet where 100 is allowed. The applicant's engineer, planner and a company representative testified, and the board's own engineer and planner commented on their reports.

The board approved the Chipotle on a roll call. The captions don't capture every vote cleanly, but every vote that came through was a yes. One member said at the end that he would have approved the restaurant even without the parking changes described in the next section, and the board's attorney confirmed that the restaurant's approval stands on its own either way. A nearby resident who spoke said his concern was not the restaurant itself but traffic. He said drivers regularly turn left out of an exit that is supposed to be right-turn-only, that traffic from the Route 33 signal backs up past Bentley Road, that cars entering Gateway Boulevard South from Applegarth Road and then turning into the restaurant could create a bottleneck, and that more housing is on the way nearby. He also said he doubted the applicant's testimony that food deliveries take only 10 minutes, and asked that the large sign be kept low. He suggested that other projects should work with the state on the traffic lights. Board members told him that traffic enforcement is a police matter, not the Zoning Board's.

3. Parking, a bike lane and a private road: the argument that took up most of the night

Most of the discussion was not about burritos at all. As part of the same application, the applicant asked for an "amended final site plan" approval to change the striping on Gateway Boulevard, a private road owned by the neighborhood's homeowners' association (HOA). Today the road has an eight-foot-wide striped bike lane that is wide enough to park a car in, and residents park there, sometimes close enough to the corners to block drivers' view. The proposal would remove the bike lane in the residential stretch and replace it with about 25 striped parallel parking spaces, mark the corners with yellow hatching so drivers can see around them, move the bike symbols into the shared lane with "shared lane" signs, and leave the existing bike lane in the commercial stretch with "no parking" signs. The applicant's representative stressed that the idea came from the HOA board and the township, not from the restaurant, and said the bike lane was made so wide because a township planner asked for it years ago. The board's engineer said he thought the plan was safer than leaving things as they are, though he said he had no better alternative to offer.

Several board members were not convinced that painting lines would change behavior. One said he wasn't sure he could vote for the parking piece, another asked why the board was involved in a private road at all, and a third asked who would enforce it. The answers were that the HOA owns the road, but that an earlier approval gave the township police authority to enforce state motor-vehicle laws (known as "Title 39") there, and that the current bike lane is not signed "no parking," which is part of the confusion. The board's attorney said the board could grant all of the relief or only some of it, and that conditions could be attached to what it granted. The motion that passed approved the Chipotle together with the roughly 25 parking spaces, with the commercial-area spaces removed, on the condition that the HOA signs off in writing, gives the board attorney documentation, and agrees to enforcement and no-parking signage. If the HOA does not approve, the parking piece falls away and the Chipotle approval stands on its own. Several members said the motion was confusing, and the attorney walked through it twice before the vote.

Also from Tuesday's meeting

An earlier decision is put in writing

The board adopted a "memorialization" for application VA-5265-26, filed by a company the captions give as 257 Appleg LLC. A memorialization is the formal written resolution that records a decision the board already made at an earlier meeting; there was no discussion and no public comment. We could not confirm the property or the nature of that application from the recording, and the township's agenda did not describe it.

Housekeeping

The board approved the minutes of its June 30 meeting, reported no correspondence and adjourned. The township's website lists the next regular meeting as October 27; there is no November meeting, and the December meeting is set for December 1.
